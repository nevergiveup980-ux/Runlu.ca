The meetings that truly change a life rarely arrive with music.

In the autumn of 1995, Zhang Yuansheng had only recently joined Oilfield Construction Company No. 3. His days were packed with reporting in, learning names, learning rules, and following senior workers out to job sites. At night in the dormitory, he could still hear the day in his head: telephones ringing, engines starting, steel pipes striking against one another. He had left school only a few months earlier, yet that life already seemed to sit behind a sheet of glass.

He had not seriously thought about marriage then. A man of twenty-four could speak grandly about “the future,” while still having no idea where he would eat dinner the next day.

Older colleagues liked teasing the younger ones. “Seeing anyone?” “When are you going to settle down?” Yuansheng usually laughed it off. “I only just started working.”

Lin Lan entered his life slowly, somewhere outside all that laughter.

Many years later, Yuansheng could no longer say exactly when he first met her. He remembered that she did not rush to speak over people. He remembered the steadiness of her gaze and the way she smiled without making a performance of it. But the colour of the table, who introduced whom, whether there had been wind outside that day—those things were gone.

Memory is selective. It keeps the person who matters and often erases the scenery around the first meeting.

They first really talked over a meal with several other young people. The table was small, the food ordinary. The conversation drifted from work to school, from someone’s child to the question of who was still single.

Someone pointed at Yuansheng and joked, “University graduate, got a job, and still can’t talk.”

Yuansheng said, “Stop trying to introduce me to people.”

Across the table, Lin Lan lowered her head and smiled. Someone asked her, “What do you think?”

She looked up at Yuansheng. “I’ve barely heard him say anything. How would I know whether he can talk?”

The table erupted. Yuansheng felt his face grow warm and replied, “Then I suppose I still haven’t said little enough.”

Lin Lan smiled again.

Years later, when he sometimes remembered that scene, he would think that perhaps a relationship does not truly begin when two people first see each other, but when, amid everyone else talking, each of them hears one particular sentence from the other.

They began seeing each other more often. Sometimes with a group, sometimes alone. There were no smartphones then, no message that could be sent at any moment. To find someone, you had to remember when she might be home and when she got off work. If no one answered the telephone, you waited.

Waiting was not a spinning circle on a screen. It was actual empty time.

One evening they had agreed to meet. Yuansheng arrived early and waited at the corner for ten minutes, then twenty. The sky was overcast and wind moved between the buildings. After more than half an hour, he began to feel foolish.

More than forty minutes later, he turned to leave. He had gone only a few dozen metres when someone called behind him, “Zhang Yuansheng!”

Lin Lan ran toward him, a fine layer of sweat on her forehead, still catching her breath. Yuansheng spoke with irritation before he could stop himself. “So you do know what time it is.”

She paused. “An old man fell on the road. A few of us took him to a nearby clinic. There wasn’t a telephone there.”

His anger suddenly had nowhere to go. “Why... why didn’t you tell me earlier?”

Lin Lan looked at him. “How was I supposed to tell you earlier?”

She asked it calmly, and he had no answer.

They still ate together that evening. No one made a formal apology, and no one announced that they now understood each other better. But Yuansheng remembered one small thing: this young woman made her own judgments, and she was not afraid of his displeasure.

That would matter later.

When something happened, Yuansheng’s instinct was to solve it first. Lin Lan often began with a different question: “Why?” Sometimes she added, “I don’t agree.”

When he became excited about an idea, she asked about the part closest to the ground. “What about the money?” “What about the time?” “You think it works. Why should everyone else think so?”

Sometimes her questions irritated him. Often, after the irritation passed, he discovered that she had a point.

Their relationship did not suddenly become intense. Young love in those years still carried a good deal of restraint. Who called first, who disappeared for several days—such things left marks in the mind, but were not always spoken aloud.

For a while Yuansheng became very busy at work and went more than ten days without properly seeing her. He thought little of it. He would see her when the work settled down. When he finally had time to find Lin Lan, she was noticeably cooler than usual.

It took him a while to understand. “You can be busy,” she said. “But at least let me know you’re busy. You can’t simply disappear for ten days.”

His first instinct was to explain. The work unit was busy. Temporary assignments kept appearing. He had only just started. Where was all this extra time supposed to come from?

Halfway through the explanation, he stopped.

Lin Lan had not asked him to appear every day. She only wanted to have a place inside his life.

It was the first time Yuansheng vaguely understood that falling in love did not mean adding one person beside the life he already had. A real relationship changed how time was divided, changed the order of decisions, even changed the subject of a sentence.

Before, his thoughts almost always began with “I”: where I want to go, what I want to do, what my future will be.

Slowly, “we” began appearing in those sentences.

Of course they argued. Once, Yuansheng spoke too bluntly in front of other people and embarrassed Lin Lan. The more they tried to explain themselves, the less they understood each other. In the end, they walked away in opposite directions.

That night he lay in bed convinced he was right. The next morning he was still convinced. By the third day, it occurred to him that being right was not quite as important as he had imagined.

He wanted to see her. But going to find her first felt too much like surrender. Young people are talented at making simple things complicated.

By afternoon he finally invented an excuse: he was “just passing by.” Lin Lan saw him and asked, “What are you doing here?”

“Passing by.”

“What a coincidence.”

“Mm.”

She turned to leave. Yuansheng hurried to say, “Wait.”

He hesitated for a few seconds. “That day... what I said was a little out of line.”

Lin Lan looked back. “Only a little?”

Yuansheng could not help laughing. “All right. Pretty far out of line.”

She laughed too.

Many years later, the things they disagreed about would become much larger: a child, immigration, a house, work, money, aging parents. Yet the way they handled disagreement may already have been taking shape on those afternoons when they were young.

It was not that they never argued. It was that after the argument, someone eventually had to come back first.

There was also a rainy evening. Yuansheng had no umbrella and stood near a doorway waiting for the rain to weaken. Lin Lan came out and saw him. “What are you waiting for?”

“For the rain to stop.”

“Does the weather take orders from you?” She shifted her umbrella aside. “Come on.”

Sharing one umbrella is awkward. Stand too close and it feels unnatural; stand too far away and one shoulder gets soaked. Yuansheng kept angling the umbrella toward her. By the time they reached the corner, half of his own shoulder was wet.

When Lin Lan noticed, she pushed the umbrella firmly back toward him. “Are ‘I’m fine’ the two words you like most?”

For a moment they were standing very close. Neither spoke. The sound of rain on the umbrella suddenly seemed unusually clear.

Young love does not always need the sentence “I like you.” Sometimes an ordinary movement becomes unexpectedly awkward, and the heart already knows why.

Later, when they met each other’s parents for the first time, both were nervous. Yuansheng was usually not short of words, but at Lin Lan’s home he sat very straight and answered each question carefully. Afterward she laughed at him. “You looked like you were reporting for duty.”

Lin Lan was just as restrained at his home. His mother clearly liked her, asking whether she wanted this dish or that. His father said little and asked only a few questions about work.

Outside, Lin Lan asked quietly, “Does your father not like me?”

Yuansheng said, “If he asked you three questions, he likes you a lot.”

“Your family’s standard is very low.”

Once love reached the stage of meeting parents, it stopped being something that belonged only to two people. Two families now knew. The word marriage no longer felt far away.

One evening they stopped their bicycles by the roadside to eat ice pops. Lin Lan suddenly asked, “What kind of home would you like to live in someday?”

“A home is enough,” Yuansheng said.

“How many rooms?”

“Enough to live in.”

Lin Lan looked at him. “Can’t you imagine anything at all?”

He thought seriously. “A kitchen of our own.”

She stared at him. “That’s it?”

Only after a moment did Yuansheng understand. “Are you asking me about getting married?”

Lin Lan glared at him. “You only just figured that out?”

Some people talk about love through poems. They talked through the idea of a future kitchen.

1996 passed quickly. Young people in the work unit married one after another. Some were assigned housing; some began calculating when they might have children. Yuansheng, too, began seriously thinking about the words “starting a family.”

Not because everyone else was doing it, and not because he had reached some prescribed age.

One day he simply realized that if he changed jobs someday, if he ever left the oilfield, if life truly pushed him somewhere farther away, he still wanted this person beside him.

When he told Lin Lan, there were no flowers and no kneeling. They were simply walking along an ordinary road.

Yuansheng said, “How about we get married?”

Lin Lan looked at him for two seconds. “Is this a proposal?”

“More or less.”

“What does ‘more or less’ mean?”

Yuansheng cleared his throat and discovered that words that normally came easily had become clumsy. In the end he said only, “I want to live the rest of my life with you.”

Lin Lan did not answer at once. She walked two steps ahead.

Yuansheng caught up. “Well? Yes or no?”

She teased him. “Aren’t you good at waiting?”

He immediately remembered the evening when he had stood at the corner for more than forty minutes.

Lin Lan finally smiled. “All right.”

That was it. No applause.

Later they also talked seriously about what came next. Lin Lan said, “When something happens, don’t decide everything by yourself.”

Yuansheng nodded. “That I can do.”

“Not ‘I can do.’ You have to remember it.”

He smiled. “You’re already starting to manage me?”

“You still have time to change your mind.”

Yuansheng looked at her and did not return the joke. “I’m not planning to.”

Lin Lan grew quiet too.

No one stood nearby to witness it. Bicycles continued along the road. In the distance someone called a child home. The city had no idea that two ordinary young people had just decided to join two separate lives somewhere along the way.

There was never any theme music in their love.

It simply made a young man, when thinking about tomorrow, stop using “I” as the only subject.

Before 1997 truly arrived, Zhang Yuansheng already knew that some of the days ahead would be lived by two people.
