In 1998, when Zhang Yuansheng heard his daughter cry for the first time, his mind was empty.
Years later he would wonder why that moment had not brought some sudden sense of “the continuation of life” or “the awakening of fatherhood,” as novels like to describe. The truth was simpler: first, he felt relief.
The baby was crying. Loudly. That meant she had strength.

The hours of waiting had pulled his nerves tight. There was little a man outside a delivery room could do: walk, sit down, stand up, ask one question, then wait again. People passed now and then, nurses’ shoes made a regular sound against the floor, and the clock on the wall moved more slowly than usual.
For the first time Yuansheng understood that when a person is truly powerless, even the word “help” can feel useless.

Lin Lan was inside. He was outside. There was no plan beyond that.
When the news came, Yuansheng stood so quickly that his knee struck the chair. He did not feel the pain.
“Mother and daughter are both fine.”
The thing that had been hanging inside him all day finally dropped.
When he truly saw his daughter, his first thought was: how can she be this small?

So small that she did not look like someone who would one day walk, talk, go to school, and argue with her parents. Her fingers were so thin he was afraid to touch them. Her face was wrinkled, and her eyes stayed closed most of the time.
Lin Lan was exhausted, but she still asked, “Who does she look like?”
Yuansheng studied the baby. “Hard to tell yet.”
“You can’t just say she looks like me?”

“What if she doesn’t later?”
Lin Lan did not even have the energy to roll her eyes.
It was the first unimportant conversation the parents had after their daughter entered the world. Their life would later be made of thousands of such unimportant conversations.
In the novel, they called their daughter Xiaoqi.
Once she had a name, the baby seemed to become a particular person rather than simply “the baby.”

Almost no one truly slept on the first night home. The moment Xiaoqi cried, Yuansheng sat up.
“Is she hungry?”
“She just ate.”
“Cold?”
“Feel her.”
“My hands are cold.”
With her eyes closed, Lin Lan said, “Then warm them first.”
At two in the morning, two adults stood around a person who could not speak and discussed causes. In the end, there might have been no cause at all.

Babies simply cry.
Yuansheng found this one of the hardest answers for an adult to accept. At work, a problem had a cause somewhere. A crying baby did not always have a cause you could identify and fix.
Sometimes all you could do was hold her.
The first time Yuansheng picked up his daughter, his body stiffened as though he were carrying something extremely fragile. In his arms, Xiaoqi cried even louder.

Lin Lan tried not to laugh. “Don’t hold her like a box.”
“Then how?”
“Support her head.”
“I am.”
“Relax your hands.”
“I’m afraid I’ll drop her.”
“The more afraid you are, the more you look like you’re going to.”
A new father’s dignity disappeared quickly in front of a baby weighing only a few kilograms.

By the second week, he had learned to carry Xiaoqi around the room in the middle of the night. Walk too slowly and she cried; a little faster and she settled.
Yuansheng paced in circles as if following a procedure.
From the bed, Lin Lan listened to his footsteps. “How many laps have you done?”
“No idea.”
“You can measure the room with a ruler next time.”

“I think twenty laps is about enough for sleep.”
“You’re already deriving a rule?”
The rule failed the next night.
Twenty laps did nothing. Forty did nothing.
For the first time Yuansheng understood in a very concrete way that a real human being was more complicated than any system.

Sometimes Xiaoqi would finally fall asleep on his shoulder and he would be afraid to put her down. His arm slowly began to ache. The room was dark, Lin Lan was asleep, and an occasional car passed outside.
In those unseen nights, Yuansheng first understood that many things he did from now on could no longer be calculated only in terms of his own gain or loss.
This little person knew nothing at all.

But she would be hungry, cold, sick, and slowly grow up.
She placed a responsibility on his shoulders without ever saying, “Please.”
As the child grew, the objects in the house changed meaning.
A table corner was no longer just a table corner. An outlet was no longer just an outlet. Hot cups, cupboard doors, chair edges—all of them became risk points.

One day Yuansheng crouched down and looked across the living room from the height of a small child.
Lin Lan asked, “What are you doing?”
“Seeing what she can reach.”
“You look like you’re inspecting a work site.”
“Pretty much.”
He moved a cup farther from the edge. Not long after, Xiaoqi’s hand reached exactly where it had been.

Lin Lan looked at him. “You were right this time.”
Yuansheng was very pleased.
Fatherhood sometimes offered very modest satisfactions: moving a cup before a child could reach it.
The first thing that truly frightened him was Xiaoqi’s fever in the middle of the night.
She had been fine during the day. At night her face turned red and her body felt wrong. The moment Lin Lan touched her, she sat up.

Yuansheng found the thermometer. When the number appeared, both of them went silent.
“Should we go to the hospital?”
“Take it again.”
“We just did.”
“You’re the one who doesn’t trust it.”
He took her temperature again. The number did not fall simply because he looked at it twice.
When they finally decided to go, he dressed in half the usual time.

It was cold outside. Xiaoqi normally cried with force, but that night she had little energy, which frightened him more.
At the hospital, there was more waiting.
Yuansheng hated waiting, especially when he did not know how serious something might be.
After examining her, the doctor said it did not look serious and told them what to do and what to watch.
Yuansheng asked twice, “She’s really okay?”

The doctor looked at him. “First child?”
Yuansheng nodded.
“You’ll get a little more used to this.”
At the time he could not believe anyone could get used to such things.
Of course, slowly they did. Children fall, cough, vomit without warning, and produce all kinds of small emergencies that make parents sit upright in the middle of the night. Growing up is, in part, a series of small disasters safely crossed.

On the way home that night, Xiaoqi slept in Lin Lan’s arms.
Lin Lan said, “You’ve become more timid since having a child.”
Yuansheng thought for a while. “It isn’t that I’m more timid. There’s just one more person who isn’t allowed to have anything happen to her.”
After he said it, both of them went quiet.
Having a daughter also made Yuansheng begin to understand his own parents from another angle.

His mother would sometimes bring up the frightening things from his childhood, especially the time in Zhaojiadian Village when he mistakenly ate castor beans. Before becoming a father, Yuansheng heard those stories mostly as evidence that he had been reckless and lucky, almost family anecdotes.
Now, listening while holding his own child, they sounded completely different.
It had not only been a child getting into trouble.

It had been two adults who had nearly lost their child.
For the first time Yuansheng could see his childhood from his parents’ position.
The understanding softened him a little.
His father still spoke hard. His mother still fussed. But he was less easily annoyed, because he had begun to understand that behind a great deal of control there was simply fear.

When Xiaoqi began trying to stand, both Yuansheng and Lin Lan were nearby.
She held the edge of the table, swaying. Lin Lan whispered, “Don’t help her.”
“She’s going to fall.”
“Let her stand.”
The child released one hand and wobbled.
Yuansheng’s arm was already reaching out, then stopped itself.

A few seconds later Xiaoqi sat down hard on the floor. She looked surprised for two seconds, then instead of crying, she laughed.
Yuansheng laughed too.
Many years later in Canada, when he watched his daughter drive away alone for the first time, he felt almost the same thing in his chest.
Wanting to help.
Knowing he had to let go.
Perhaps becoming a father is learning that lesson again and again.

One weekend Yuansheng had plans to go out with friends. Just before he left, Xiaoqi suddenly became unwell.
Lin Lan did not ask him to stay.
He stood at the door for a few seconds, then took off the shoes he had just put on.
“Didn’t you have plans?”
“I’m not going.”
“I can manage.”
“I know.”
“Then why stay?”

Yuansheng looked at the child. “Don’t feel like going.”
That was not entirely true.
He did want to go.
Something else had simply become more important.
Responsibility does not always require a grand act of will. Sometimes it is only this: you stand at the door, and in the end, you change your shoes back.
As Xiaoqi learned to speak, the sound of the household changed too.

The first time she clearly said “Dad,” Yuansheng froze for two seconds.
“Say it again.”
Xiaoqi looked at him and refused.
He coaxed her. “Say Dad.”
She only smiled.
Lin Lan said, “Stop pressuring her.”
“She just said it.”
“Once isn’t enough?”
“No.”
A little later, Xiaoqi said it again on her own.

This time Yuansheng did not ask for a repeat.
Once someone calls you “Dad,” the grammar of life changes.
Before, the future belonged mainly to you.
After that, every sentence about housing, work, money, or distant places contained one more person.
Yuansheng did not yet know how concrete that fact would soon become.

A few years later, when “Canada”—a word once as distant as the edge of a map—was finally placed on the table as a real possibility, he would ask himself again and again: should we go?
On the surface, the question was about a country.
In truth, the first person he looked at was his daughter.
