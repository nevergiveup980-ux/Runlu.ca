Only when the preparations truly began did Zhang Yuansheng understand that the two words “going abroad” turned, in real life, into stack after stack of paper.

Birth, education, employment, family, income—each part required proof. A life already lived suddenly had to be retold to another system through dates, stamps, and documents.

Lin Lan had more patience for such details. “Are you sure about this date?” “More or less.” “Can you write ‘more or less’ on the form?” she asked. Yuansheng had to go looking again.

One document was rejected over a small detail, and he made the trip twice. When he came back the second time, he dropped the papers onto the table with a dark expression.

Lin Lan asked, “Giving up?” Yuansheng said, “Who said I’m giving up? I’m annoyed, not stopping.” She smiled. “That sounds like you.”

They never treated the application as a heroic undertaking. Most of the time it was forms, waiting, more documents, and more waiting.

The hardest part was not any single form. It was uncertainty. They did not know whether they would be approved, when they would hear, or whether, once permission finally came, they would still dare to leave.

For a while, Yuansheng genuinely considered letting the whole thing go. Work was going well, their daughter was settling into her world, and dismantling a life already assembled only to rebuild it somewhere unfamiliar did not look like an easy choice.

“What if we don’t go?” he said to Lin Lan one day.

She was folding Xiaoqi’s clothes and did not look up. “If you truly don’t want to go, then don’t. But don’t say you never wanted it just because you’re afraid it might go badly.”

Yuansheng looked at her and said nothing for a long moment.

Then Lin Lan added, “You’re not the only one who has spent years on this. I want to know what the world outside is like too.”

From then on, it was no longer only Yuansheng’s curiosity. It became a choice the two of them would carry together.

Their parents were another real threshold. The first time Yuansheng spoke seriously about leaving, his mother’s first question was, “That far away—how often will you come back?”

He had no answer.

His father asked, “What about your job?” Yuansheng said, “If we really go, I’ll have to give it up.” His father was quiet for a while, then said only, “You’re not by yourself anymore.”

Yuansheng understood the weight of the sentence. His wife and child were inside his decision. A man may accept the cost of his own curiosity, but he cannot pretend that his family pays nothing.

The final decision did not arrive all at once on a particular day. It formed slowly through repeated doubt, pauses, and the act of taking the papers out again.

Lin Lan was afraid too—of language, work, their child failing to adjust, and the distance from their parents. She asked, “What if we go and discover we were wrong?”

Yuansheng was about to say, “There will always be a way,” but stopped. For the first time he admitted that some roads do not come with answers waiting at the far end.

A few days later they sat at the table again. Yuansheng said, “If we don’t go, I may always wonder why we never tried.”

Lin Lan asked, “And if we go and regret it?”

“Then at least we’ll know.”

Lin Lan looked down at the documents and finally said three words: “Then we try.”

When the approval finally came, neither of them was as excited as they had imagined. Something awaited for so long became a checklist the moment it turned real: when to leave, how to arrange the money, where to stay first, what to do about Xiaoqi, when to resign.

May 2003 drew closer. Yuansheng began preparing to end his years at Oilfield Construction Company No. 3. Roads he had taken every day, the place where he sat, the people he ate with—all began to carry the feeling of “the last time.”

At a farewell gathering someone joked, “Don’t forget us when you get rich overseas.” Yuansheng laughed too. “Forget getting rich. I don’t even know whether I’ll find a job.”

Once his father accepted that the decision was real, he did not suddenly begin praising Canada. He simply knew his son had chosen. One night when neither of them was asleep, he said, “Don’t pretend to be stronger than you are. If it truly doesn’t work, come back. Home is here.”

Before leaving, they went back once more to Zhaojiadian Village. Roads and houses had changed so much that many childhood places existed only when his mother pointed and said what used to be there.

Standing there, Yuansheng understood that leaving never freezes a place until your return. Next time, the place will be different, and so will you.

His father helped weigh the luggage and objected to one suitcase being packed too tightly. “Even if it’s within the limit, don’t fill it to the edge. Leave a little room.” Yuansheng listened without arguing.

His mother’s worries were larger than any suitcase. “Take this too.” “We can buy it there.” “Take it anyway.”

Xiaoqi worried only about her toys. “Can’t this one come?” Lin Lan said it was too big. Xiaoqi hugged it and asked, “Will it be scared at home by itself?” The adults laughed, then fell a little quiet.

At last, the airline tickets lay on the table.

The world was no longer simply outside. It had a direction.

Canada.

And Zhang Yuansheng, Lin Lan, and their daughter were going.
