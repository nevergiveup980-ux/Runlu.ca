After the child learned to walk, the home suddenly became more dangerous than before.

Table corners were dangerous.

Electrical outlets were dangerous.

The kettle was dangerous.

Even a cup an adult casually left beside the sofa was dangerous.

For the first time, Zhang Yuansheng realized that a room that looked ordinary to adults could become a maze full of traps to a child who had only just learned to walk.

His daughter moved from one end of the wall to the other with one hand against it, and Yuansheng’s eyes followed every step.

“Stop staring at her all the time,” Lin Lan said.

“What if she falls when I’m not looking?”

“Then she falls and gets back up.”

“Easy for you to say.”

Lin Lan glanced at him.

“Didn’t you fall when you were little?”

Yuansheng thought of the ditches, walls, trees, and water around Zhaojiadian village.

Fall? More than once.

A few times he had nearly lost the chance to get back up at all.

He smiled and finally pulled his hand back.

His daughter took three steps. On the fourth, she fell exactly as expected.

She started crying.

Yuansheng reached for her almost at once.

Lin Lan sighed beside him. “You’re more nervous than she is.”

That was how their days began to take shape: through sounds like these.

Who got up first. Who washed first. Who woke the child. What they would eat for breakfast. Which clothes their daughter would wear. Yuansheng went to work; Lin Lan had her own things to do. At dinner, the conversation was rarely about national affairs. It was about someone they had met, a new word their daughter had learned, or something the household needed to buy.

Once life becomes stable, it is easy to imagine that the future will simply keep extending in the same shape.

Yuansheng believed that for a while.

At the oilfield there was work, a home, parents and relatives nearby, familiar roads. The work-unit system was changing, of course. The market was becoming more visible. But an ordinary young man did not wake every morning thinking about history.

History rarely changes ordinary people through headlines first.

It changes how wages are paid, how jobs are found, how housing is assigned, how things are bought. Only later does it change what a person thinks the word “stability” means.

By the late 1990s, new things were appearing everywhere.

Shops carried more goods than before.

There were more advertisements on the streets.

Cities on television seemed brighter every year.

Some people left state jobs to start businesses. Some quit their work units. Some came back from the south saying money seemed to move faster down there.

Young people at work began talking about stocks, computers, and the Internet.

The first time Yuansheng really used a computer, he did not feel anything like a call from destiny.

The monitor was bulky. The machine started slowly. Many operations required memorizing commands, and one wrong entry could send you somewhere you did not understand.

But he liked it.

Not because computers seemed magical.

Because they followed rules.

Give the right instruction and the machine carried it out.

Give the wrong instruction and it would show you the wrong result without mercy.

He liked that directness.

He could not have imagined that more than twenty years later, sitting at a computer late at night in Canada, he would build websites, invoice systems, and a warehouse operating system with the same instinct: try it first and see what happens.

Interests that later seem to appear suddenly usually showed themselves much earlier.

At the time, no one had given them a name.

When the family bought its first slightly expensive appliance, the two of them discussed it for days.

“We’ll have to buy one sooner or later,” Yuansheng said.

“Sooner or later isn’t the same as now,” Lin Lan replied.

“Money sitting there doesn’t grow by itself.”

“It definitely won’t grow after you spend it.”

They wrote their income and expenses on paper.

The child.

Daily life.

Things for their parents.

Money that had to remain untouched for emergencies.

In the end, they bought it.

When the appliance was carried into the apartment, both of them were happy.

The satisfaction of an ordinary family is often very concrete.

A television.

A refrigerator.

Furniture that fits better.

A new pair of shoes for a child.

None of these things appears in anyone’s biography, yet together they form a family’s judgment that life is “getting better.”

After their daughter began to talk, she reinvented the language of the house as well.

When she could not pronounce a word, she replaced it with a sound of her own. The adults thought it was cute and began using it too. Soon the whole family was using a word that no one else could understand, until years later they realized none of them remembered when the word had disappeared.

Childhood is full of disappearances like that.

The bottle disappeared.

The little bed disappeared.

The habit of needing to be held before sleep disappeared.

One day she no longer needed her father to hold her hand for every step.

At the time, each of those things felt permanent.

Yuansheng became more experienced at work.

Processes that once required reminders became things he could perform without thinking. Newer employees arrived, and he found himself explaining the rules to them.

For the first time, he understood that time had passed through him too.

He was no longer the young graduate who had just left university.

When someone at work called him “Old Zhang,” he would still turn around to see whether they meant someone else.

“How am I old?”

“You’re married and you’ve got a kid. You’re not old?”

“Then what are you?”

“I’m a senior.”

Everyone laughed.

Back then, thirty still seemed like a distant threshold.

Yuansheng believed that people in their thirties should already have figured life out.

When he approached thirty himself, he discovered that nobody had.

Adults were only better than children at pretending they knew what came next.

Around the year 2000, the world seemed to accelerate.

Foreign cities appeared constantly in the news. More people went abroad to study, work, or visit family. Countries that had once existed only on maps became concrete through acquaintances.

“So-and-so went to Australia.”

“Someone’s relative is in the United States.”

“What’s Canada like?”

The first time that last question entered Yuansheng’s life, he paid little attention.

Canada was too far away.

Too far to have anything to do with his daily life.

But once a word has been spoken once, it is never entirely unfamiliar the second time.

An old classmate later moved abroad. After a while, news came back. It was not a story about getting rich, and no one claimed the streets were paved with gold. Only that the environment was different, the rhythm of life was different, the beginning was hard, and after a while a person could manage.

Yuansheng listened.

Someone asked him, “Have you ever thought about going abroad?”

“To do what?”

“Live a different kind of life.”

Yuansheng laughed.

“What’s wrong with the life I have?”

Nothing, really.

His job was stable.

His family was stable.

His daughter was healthy.

His parents were nearby.

If life were a report card, he already had many of the answers people wanted.

But some ideas do not begin in dissatisfaction.

Sometimes they begin in curiosity.

What if there were another way to live?

Yuansheng told no one about the thought.

It was too light. Saying it aloud would have made it sound like an irresponsible joke.

Until one day Lin Lan brought it up herself.

“Have you been reading about other countries lately?”

Yuansheng looked up.

“How do you know?”

“Those papers on your desk are nearly worn out from being handled.”

They were materials about life in Canada and immigration policy, gathered from everywhere: photocopies from acquaintances, newspaper clippings, pages passed along by friends.

Yuansheng smiled.

“Just looking.”

“How long have you been ‘just looking’?”

“Not long.”

“When you say not long, it usually means quite a while.”

Lin Lan picked up the papers.

“Do you really want to go?”

The question had finally moved from inside his head onto the table.

Yuansheng did not answer immediately.

Their daughter was playing nearby.

Someone rode a bicycle past the window.

The kettle clicked in the kitchen.

The most important decisions in a family often begin against ordinary backgrounds like this.

“I don’t know,” Yuansheng said.

Lin Lan looked at him.

“If you don’t know, that means you’ve thought about it.”

“Thinking about it doesn’t mean we’re going.”

“Then think it through.”

She did not say yes or no.

That made Yuansheng take the idea more seriously.

An idea that belongs only to you can be abandoned at any time. Once another person begins asking about it, the idea gains weight.

That night, for the first time, they really talked about “outside.”

What about language?

What about work?

What would they do after arriving?

What about their parents?

What about the child?

What if they failed?

By the end, even Yuansheng felt there seemed to be more risks than opportunities.

Lin Lan asked, “Then why do you still want to?”

He was quiet for a long time.

“Maybe I just want to know whether I can still live another way.”

Lin Lan did not laugh.

She put the papers back on the table.

“Then first find out how we would go.”

That sentence changed nothing about the next morning.

Yuansheng went to work as usual.

Lin Lan went about her day as usual.

Their daughter kept growing as usual.

But from that night on, an invisible road had appeared inside their home.

During those years, Yuansheng and Lin Lan began to own a few things that felt truly theirs as a family.

Not a borrowed table. Not bedding given by relatives at their wedding. A television they had discussed and chosen together.

The day they brought it home, Yuansheng carried the box upstairs while Lin Lan followed behind.

“Slow down. Don’t drop it the day we buy it.”

“I’ve carried carpet without dropping it.”

“This is a television, not carpet.”

Yuansheng thought she was making too much of it.

But when they finally plugged it in and the screen lit up, both of them sat there watching a little longer than necessary.

Xiaoqi excitedly pressed every button on the remote.

A family’s sense of prosperity can be almost childish.

A new appliance, a better set of bowls, a table that truly belongs to you—each can make you feel that life is moving forward.

On weekends they sometimes walked through shopping malls without necessarily buying anything.

Xiaoqi would stop at the toy section and refuse to leave.

At first Yuansheng softened easily.

“Let’s buy one.”

“We already have toys at home,” Lin Lan said.

“This one is different.”

“You’re better at making excuses than she is.”

Once a child begins to want things, parents face the question of how much to give.

Yuansheng had grown up with very little and worried that his daughter might be denied things she wanted.

Lin Lan worried more about what would happen if everything came too easily.

Between them, a balance formed.

Not never.

Not every time she asked.

Once, Xiaoqi cried in a store because they would not buy a toy.

Yuansheng felt the eyes of other people and wanted to buy it quickly so they could leave.

Lin Lan crouched beside the child.

“We’re not buying it today. You can cry. When you’re finished, we’ll go home.”

Yuansheng stared at her.

On the way home he said, “You can really stand it.”

Lin Lan replied, “I can stand her crying. What you can’t stand is other people watching you.”

She had caught him exactly.

For the first time he realized that many concessions parents make to children are really attempts to relieve their own embarrassment.

Family life is made of mirrors like that.

Children reflect their parents.

Husbands and wives reflect each other.

Yuansheng became more competent at work, yet at home he still made simple mistakes.

He forgot anniversaries.

He promised to buy something and forgot.

A form from Xiaoqi’s school sat on the table for two days waiting for a signature.

Sometimes Lin Lan said, “You can remember dozens of carpet rolls at work but not one sheet of paper at home.”

Yuansheng defended himself. “Work has a system.”

“Then build yourself a system for home.”

At the time, she meant it as sarcasm.

Years later, their home really would depend more and more on calendars, phone reminders, and shared information.

Technology did not make a family cold.

Sometimes it simply helped a person with limited memory forget a little less.

They bought their first computer during those years too.

It was heavy and slow.

Booting took time.

Dial-up Internet made a string of noises that now sound ancient.

Xiaoqi thought the sound was funny.

When Yuansheng registered his first email address, he spent a long time choosing a username.

Many simple things feel formal the first time you do them.

Years later, when he had so many accounts he could barely remember the passwords, he would laugh at how seriously he had taken that first one.

The Internet brought the world into the home in a way that did not require travel.

Yuansheng read news, searched for information, and occasionally visited overseas websites.

Canada was no longer just a country in a newspaper.

There were photographs.

City guides.

Stories from strangers on immigration forums.

Some said life was wonderful after they moved.

Some regretted it.

Some said, Don’t come.

Some said, Come as early as you can.

The more Yuansheng read, the more he understood that other people could not answer for him.

The same country could become an entirely different life for two different people.

That lesson later shaped the way he read opinions on the Internet.

More comments do not necessarily mean more answers.

In the end, you still return to yourself.

When his mother learned that they had begun reading about moving abroad, she did not take it too seriously at first.

“Looking doesn’t hurt.”

His father did not think they would actually leave.

Perhaps Yuansheng did not fully believe it either.

When life feels complete, it is hard to imagine dismantling it by choice.

But after Xiaoqi started kindergarten, Yuansheng increasingly found himself thinking in terms of “her future.”

Before, immigration had meant asking whether he wanted to start over.

Now there was another question: could his daughter have another possibility?

That reason was not automatically right.

Canada would not automatically give a child a good life.

But it was enough to make a man who had not wanted to move begin moving.

When Xiaoqi was two or three, the family finally grew a steady rhythm out of the chaos of infancy.

Who got up first.

Who took her out.

What they ate at night.

Where they went on weekends.

What money was set aside as soon as the salary came in.

When it had been only Yuansheng and Lin Lan, changing plans at the last minute was easy. With Xiaoqi, simply preparing to leave the house could turn “let’s go out” into a project.

Once the three of them had already gone downstairs when Yuansheng realized he had forgotten something.

He went back up.

Then Lin Lan realized they had forgotten the child’s water cup.

Up again.

The third time they finally got into the car, Xiaoqi said, “I need to pee.”

Yuansheng rested his forehead on the steering wheel.

Lin Lan laughed until she could barely speak.

“This is what a family of three is.”

The house slowly filled with things.

When the first appliance that truly felt like an upgrade arrived, Yuansheng studied the manual as if it were industrial equipment.

Lin Lan said, “Can’t you just plug it in?”

“Read first.”

“You didn’t study the manual this long for the fan.”

“This one was expensive.”

The meaning of money was changing too.

When he first started working, receiving a salary meant, most simply, that he finally had money of his own.

After marriage, money became an arrangement between two people.

After a child, money acquired a relationship to time.

Money not spent this month might become tuition later.

A little more saved today might become part of a home in the future.

They had no complicated family financial model.

They simply kept track.

Yuansheng liked numbers and sometimes listed a period of expenses on paper.

Lin Lan would see the page crowded with figures.

“What are you calculating?”

“Where the money went.”

“It went where it went.”

“We should know.”

“Will knowing bring it back?”

“No, but next time maybe less of it goes there.”

His attention to where things flowed would later extend to inventory, investments, and system logs.

A person rarely turns suddenly into someone else.

Many later “methods” were practiced first inside a small household.

Once Xiaoqi could speak in full sentences, the family acquired a third opinion.

In stores, she pointed at toys and asked for them.

Yuansheng hated it when she cried.

It was not only because he felt sorry for her.

When other people looked at them in public, he became uncomfortable.

Once Xiaoqi sat on the floor crying because they had refused to buy something.

Yuansheng said quietly, “Maybe we should just buy it.”

Lin Lan looked at him. “For her, or for you?”

“What do you mean?”

“You can’t stand people watching.”

Again, she was right.

Lin Lan crouched beside Xiaoqi.

“We’re not buying it today. You can cry. When you’re done, we’ll go.”

The child really cried.

Lin Lan really waited.

Yuansheng stood there feeling worse than the child.

After a few minutes, Xiaoqi stood up on her own.

They bought nothing.

Outside the store, Yuansheng said, “You’re tough.”

“If she learns that crying buys things, she’ll cry longer next time.”

Yuansheng nodded.

Years later, while building software, the principle “don’t reward a broken process” would feel strangely familiar to him.

A family is a system too.

Every concession trains the next behavior.

Of course, they were not so strict that there was no joy.

When the weather was good, they took Xiaoqi out on weekends.

A child’s standard for whether a place is fun is completely different from an adult’s.

Adults might see an ordinary open space; she could chase pigeons there for half a day.

Adults might carefully plan a destination; ten minutes later she might say she was tired.

Yuansheng gradually learned to plan a little less.

The days a family remembers are often not the “best” ones.

Once it suddenly rained while they were out. The three of them stood under an awning eating something, and years later they still remembered it.

Xiaoqi dropped a piece of food and cried as if something terrible had happened.

Yuansheng gave her his.

Lin Lan said, “You don’t have to give her everything.”

“This isn’t a matter of principle.”

“You have plenty of principles.”

“The necessary ones.”

Through teasing like that, the days of the household became visible and solid.

A table stopped being a new table and acquired scratches.

A wall kept the faint mark of a child’s drawing that had been rubbed away.

Cupboards filled with medicine, documents, photographs, and small things no one could bring themselves to throw out.

A house does not become a home when the decorating is finished.

It becomes a home after it has been used enough to show wear.

Life at work stabilized too.

Yuansheng was no longer the new employee who had arrived in oversized work clothes on his first day.

People came to him with questions.

Some procedures had become so familiar he knew the next step without thinking.

Stability gave safety, but it also quietly created an illusion: perhaps life would now continue more or less like this.

Yuansheng, still in his twenties, did not think there was anything wrong with “like this.”

He had work.

A wife.

A daughter.

His parents nearby.

Friends nearby too.

If life were a form, the major boxes were already filled in.

That was exactly why the idea of “going abroad,” when it first entered the household, seemed so unreasonable.

Lin Lan’s first reaction was not excitement.

It was: “Why?”

At the time, Yuansheng did not yet have an answer.

He only sensed that the world was larger than the life they already possessed.

And once that feeling appears, it is difficult to pretend you never heard it.

There was no signpost yet.

No plane ticket.

They did not even know whether they would truly leave.

But their days had already taken shape.

Now, beyond that shape, a blank space had appeared.
