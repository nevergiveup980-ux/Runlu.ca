Airports are among the worst places for saying goodbye.

They are too bright, too clean, too busy. Flights are announced again and again, luggage carts cut through the crowd, and the things people truly want to say are pushed aside by practical questions: Do you have the passports? Don’t forget that bag. Keep an eye on the child.

In May 2003, standing in the departure hall with Lin Lan and their daughter, Zhang Yuansheng felt for the first time that something prepared for years was only now actually happening.

There was more luggage than they had imagined. His mother had found one last corner to fill, Lin Lan had weighed everything again the night before, and Xiaoqi hugged a toy she refused to check, looking from her grandparents to the airplanes beyond the glass.

“Are we really taking that one?” she asked.

“We’ll check the ticket in a minute,” Yuansheng said.

She knew Canada was far away, but did not understand that “far” meant an ocean between her and her grandparents, or that Spring Festival might sometimes become voices over a telephone.

His mother had been talking since they entered the airport. “Call as soon as you arrive.” “Put more clothes on her if she’s cold.” “Don’t save money on everything.”

His father said very little. He noticed a loose strap around one suitcase and crouched to tighten it. Yuansheng crouched too. “I’ll do it.”

“Leave it.” His father pulled the buckle tight and patted the case. “There.”

Many fathers do not say that they cannot bear to see you go. They tighten the luggage strap instead.

After check-in, the family had to move inward step by step. With every line crossed, the people behind them became a little farther away.

At the final barrier, his mother’s eyes were red. His father said, “Go on.”

Yuansheng wanted to say, “Don’t worry,” but the sentence felt too large. He did not know what Canada would be like himself. What right did he have to promise other people calm?

In the end he said only, “Take care of yourselves. I’ll call when we arrive.”

Xiaoqi waved. “Goodbye, Grandpa! Goodbye, Grandma!”

Yuansheng walked several steps and still turned back. His father remained where he was; his mother wiped her eyes. The image stayed in his memory for years.

The ticket was one-way.

He had left home before, left his parents for university, left school for work. Each of those departures contained an unspoken assumption: coming back would be easy.

This one did not.

When the aircraft lifted off, Xiaoqi pressed herself to the window. The city grew small, roads turned into lines, and clouds quickly covered the ground.

Yuansheng felt none of the excitement he had expected. He was simply tired. Years of applications, preparations, arguments, and waiting had ended. What remained was a more practical question: what would they do after landing?

Lin Lan said quietly, “First a place to live. Then work. Then all the paperwork.”

Yuansheng smiled. “Look at you. Making lists already.”

“I learned from you,” she said.

After they crossed the Pacific, English announcements were no longer textbook English. They were reality that had to be understood. Staff spoke far faster than recordings, and when Yuansheng missed a question, he could only say, “Sorry?”

It became one of the first words he grew fluent in after arriving in Canada.

Documents were stamped, luggage pushed onward, and the automatic doors opened to a breath of cool air.

This was Canada.

No welcoming ceremony. No camera. Only the exit of an unfamiliar city’s airport.

For the time being, they moved into a small basement suite. The windows sat near the ceiling, showing only people’s shoes and a narrow piece of sky. Their boxes from China stood in the room like an entire former life compressed into limited volume.

Their first meal was simple. Xiaoqi was so tired she began falling asleep halfway through. Yuansheng called his parents.

His mother asked, “You arrived?” “Yes.” “You’re settled?” “Yes.” “How is it?”

Yuansheng looked at the low basement window and said, “Pretty good.”

The words were not completely factual, but neither were they meant as deception. He knew that describing every awkward and unfamiliar detail to people thousands of kilometres away would solve nothing and only add worry.

His father took the phone and said only, “Getting there is enough. First get life running.”

Jet lag woke Yuansheng in the middle of the night. For several seconds he did not know where he was. An unfamiliar refrigerator hummed outside the room; Lin Lan was awake too.

They said very little. After a while, Lin Lan touched his arm. “We’re here now.”

“Yeah.”

The next morning the three of them walked outside for the first time as “new immigrants.” Xiaoqi watched lawns, parked cars, and strangers’ dogs. Yuansheng was memorizing the bus stop, the supermarket, the bank, and the streets.

A man in his early thirties had already built work, family, and an entire network of familiar relationships in China. Here he was suddenly a beginner who had to start with bus routes.

It was not a demotion. It was not an inspirational story either. It was simply a fact.

A few days later he went to the bank. The employee asked several questions. He understood the first half of one sentence and lost a word in the second. People were waiting behind him, and he felt his face grow warm.

The transaction was eventually completed. Outside, he stood on the sidewalk for a moment. Lin Lan asked, “What’s wrong?”

“I just discovered my English is worse than I thought.”

Lin Lan laughed. “I figured that out earlier. My English isn’t good either, so I never had the misunderstanding.”

They laughed together on an unfamiliar street. Awkwardness becomes lighter once two people can laugh at it together.

Then came work, telephones, school, identification numbers, and one procedure after another. Each completed task received a line through it on the list.

Their old life had not vanished. It simply could not be exchanged directly for work, credit, and familiarity here. They had to prove themselves again, piece by piece, inside a new system.

Only later did Yuansheng understand that Canada in 2003 had not handed him a brand-new life.

It had removed the outer shell of his old one and asked him the same question again: in a place where almost nothing was familiar, could he still make a life work for a family of three?

He had truly reached the faraway place.

The world did not become a dream.

It simply became larger.

And Zhang Yuansheng became small again.
