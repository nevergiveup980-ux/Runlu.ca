In the spring of 1997, Zhang Yuansheng discovered for the first time that getting married was far more complicated than deciding to get married.
A decision needed only one sentence.
A life together began with a bed, a table, and a few bowls.

Both families began discussing the date: who would notify whom, which relatives should be invited, how the room should be fixed up, where the photographs would be taken, how the banquet would be arranged. Everyone thought they were offering only one small suggestion. Small suggestions accumulated quickly enough to leave two young people barely able to breathe.
Yuansheng had assumed that the most important thing about marriage was simply that two people wanted it.

He soon learned that in a Chinese family, a wedding never belonged to only two people.
Parents had their sense of propriety. Relatives had their customs. Friends had their celebration. Even the work unit had its courtesies.
Lin Lan was more patient than he was. “Let the older generation do it the way they want,” she said. “We shouldn’t quarrel ourselves apart over things like this.”

Yuansheng nodded.
A few days later, the two of them were annoyed with each other in a furniture store.
Yuansheng liked a dark-coloured table. It was solid, the size was right, and the price was good. He walked around it and pressed a hand against the edge. “This one is fine.”
Lin Lan stood beside a lighter table. “The room is small already. Put something that dark in it and it’ll look even darker.”

“A table is for eating, not for looking at the colour.”
“You look at it every day.”
“It costs that much more.”
“We’ll use it for years.”
They stood there, neither convincing the other. A salesperson nearby became extraordinarily absorbed in straightening merchandise.
Outside the shop Lin Lan said, “Forget it. Buy yours.” Her voice was very calm.

Yuansheng walked another dozen steps, then stopped. “Go back.”
“What for?”
“To buy the one you like.”
“I don’t want it now.”
“I’ve decided you have a point.”
Lin Lan looked at him. “You’re afraid I’ll keep talking about it when we get home, aren’t you?”
Yuansheng thought for a moment. “That too.”
Lin Lan laughed.
They used that table for many years.

Only later did Yuansheng understand that marriage training might begin in a furniture store: in one home, one person’s idea of what was “reasonable” could not always be the only one.
The day they registered their marriage was quieter than either of them had imagined.

A few forms, a few confirmations, two names written where they belonged. The clerk had seen too many young couples like them to look up simply because two lives were changing direction that day.
Outside, Lin Lan looked down at the certificate in her hand. “That’s it?”
“What else did you expect?”
“I thought I’d feel something.”

“What do you feel now?”
She considered it. “Hungry.”
They went to eat, laughing.
Years later Yuansheng still remembered that moment. Great occasions arrive, and life reminds you that the next meal still has to be eaten.
The wedding itself was noisy enough.

Before dawn, people were already moving through the house. Doors opened and closed. The kettle boiled again and again. Candy, sunflower seeds, and cigarettes sat on the table. Relatives who usually exchanged little more than a nod suddenly became busy people, each apparently certain where to stand and what to say.
Someone hurried Yuansheng into his clothes. Someone adjusted his collar.

Someone told him to comb his hair again. Another person laughed: “Never seen you this nervous.”
“I’m not nervous.”
“You don’t even know where to put your hands.”
Yuansheng looked down at them and put them in his pockets. “There. Problem solved.”
Everyone laughed.

He could not see what was happening on Lin Lan’s side. Years later she told him that she had actually been unusually quiet that morning. Everyone around her was busy while she sat in front of a mirror, and for a moment the whole thing felt unreal.
Yesterday she had still been one person.
From today on, people would introduce her with another identity.
Wife.

Many details of the wedding faded. Yuansheng could no longer remember who sat at which table, which dish came first, or who offered which blessing.
He remembered the crowd, the noise, and glasses touching one another again and again.
His father said very little.

Men of that generation rarely showed softness on occasions like this. His father kept moving, checking whether the guests had been looked after and whether everything that needed doing had been done. When he finally had a moment, he stood a little way off and looked at his son.
Only once.
Yuansheng understood much later that there had probably been many words inside that look.
His mother, meanwhile, kept asking whether Lin Lan had eaten.

The bride is often the busiest person at a wedding and the one least likely to get a meal. His mother put some food in Lin Lan’s bowl. “Don’t spend all your time looking after everyone else.”
Lin Lan called her, “Mom.”
His mother paused for half a second.
Then she smiled.
Some forms of address redraw the boundaries between two families the moment they are spoken.
When the guests finally left that night, the house fell suddenly quiet.

The whole day had been packed with sound. Now there were only the two of them. Lin Lan sat on the edge of the bed, removing things from her hair one by one. Yuansheng took off his jacket and let out a long breath.
“Finally over.”
Lin Lan looked up. “What’s over?”
He caught himself and laughed. “The wedding. The marriage starts now.”

“That’s better.”
The next day, marriage revealed no grand meaning at all.
There were cups and plates to clear, borrowed things to return, new quilts and bedding to find room for, and the mystery of why one person’s belongings occupied half a cupboard.
Lin Lan also discovered that when Yuansheng said, “I’ll put this here for now,” it often meant the object would still be there two days later.

Yuansheng discovered in return that squeezing toothpaste from the middle or from the end could apparently be a serious subject.
“Isn’t it neater to squeeze from the bottom?” Lin Lan asked.
“It all comes out in the end.”
“Then why do your socks have to be paired?”
He thought about it. “That’s different.”
“How?”
He had no answer.

Real union is completed in details with no epic quality at all.
Yuansheng disliked bringing work troubles home. When Lin Lan asked, “How was today?” he often said, “Fine.”
“Fine good or fine bad?”
“Just fine.”
Eventually she learned not to press.

And Yuansheng gradually learned that when Lin Lan said, “Nothing’s wrong,” sometimes something was.
There was no textbook for this language.
They could learn it only by living under the same roof, one sentence at a time.
Their first real stretch of cold silence began when Yuansheng promised to help Lin Lan with something on the weekend and work suddenly called him in.
To him, there was no choice.

But her plans had already been disrupted several times by last-minute changes.
“You always say there’s no choice.”
“If work calls me suddenly, what am I supposed to do?”
“You can tell them you already have plans.”
“Everyone else is there and I’m supposed to leave?”
Lin Lan looked at him. “So I’m always the thing that comes last?”
Yuansheng did not know how to answer.

They spoke very little for two days.
On the second evening, there was still food on the table. Yuansheng ate and washed the dishes.
On the third morning, he got up early and made a terrible breakfast. The eggs were overcooked and the porridge too thick.
Lin Lan sat down and looked at it. “What’s this?”
“Reconciliation.”
“Reconciliation tastes this bad?”

“First attempt. The technology isn’t mature yet.”
Lin Lan finally laughed.
They did not solve the enormous question of whether work or family came first.
But they made a rule: give warning as early as possible when plans change; and if a change truly cannot be avoided, do not treat the other person’s disappointment as a failure to support your work.

Their first Spring Festival after marriage brought a new problem.
Both sets of parents wanted the children home.
There was only one holiday.
Yuansheng sat at the table with a sheet of paper and began dividing the time. Lin Lan watched.
“You’re actually making a schedule?”
“That way nobody gets missed.”
“New Year isn’t a shift roster.”
“It’s clearer if you schedule it.”

Lin Lan shook her head, then sat down and helped him calculate.
Marriage often works like that: if one person’s ridiculous method reduces arguments, it slowly becomes the household method.
They argued about money too.
Not because there was none.
Because of how to spend it.
Their old refrigerator had begun making louder and louder noises, sometimes cracking suddenly in the middle of the night.

Lin Lan said, “Let’s replace it.”
Yuansheng said, “It still works.”
“Scaring me awake every night counts as working?”
“It isn’t broken.”
She looked at him. “Do you think everything has to keep going forever as long as it can still somehow keep going?”
Yuansheng had no immediate reply.
The sentence was not only about a refrigerator.
If something still worked, he did not replace it.

If his body could take it, he did not rest. If a problem could still be postponed, he left it.
He called that thrift and endurance.
Lin Lan saw the other side: dealing with some things early could save more in the long run.
In the end, they bought another refrigerator.
When the old one was carried out, Yuansheng stood at the door watching for a while.
Lin Lan laughed. “Do you want to say goodbye to it?”

“We used it a long time.”
“When you get old, am I supposed to say you still work too?”
Yuansheng laughed. “I’ll try to keep my maintenance costs low.”
China was changing in 1997 as well.

Images of Hong Kong’s handover entered homes across the country through television. People at work talked about it; people on the street talked about it. Yuansheng watched the lights on the screen and could not possibly imagine that only a few years later he would be standing on the soil of another country.
His world was still small then.
Work. Home. Parents. Friends.
But small did not mean thin.

One evening, while they were tidying the room, Lin Lan found a wedding photograph. Yuansheng stood very straight in it, smiling a little stiffly.
“Look at you,” she said.
“What about me?”
“You look like the work unit is taking your Model Employee photo.”
Yuansheng studied it. “And you?”
“I look good.”
“You always think you look good.”

Lin Lan snatched the photograph back. “Because I do.”
They put it away again.
Neither knew that a year later there would be a child outside the frame.
By the end of 1997, marriage no longer seemed to them like the enormous event it had at the beginning of the year.
It had quickly become life itself.
Rushing to work in the morning.

Talking at night about whether there was enough food in the house.
Setting aside necessary expenses when the pay arrived.
Arguing sometimes.
Sometimes bringing home something small for the other person without planning to.
Most happiness was never photographed.
It lived in the evenings when nobody raised a camera.
For Zhang Yuansheng, the real change of 1997 was not the wedding.

It was that from this year on, he was no longer only his parents’ son or a young employee in his work unit.
He had a home of his own.
And once a person has a home, every later decision about distant places becomes more difficult.
And more worth making.
