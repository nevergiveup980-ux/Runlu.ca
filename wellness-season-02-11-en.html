How Age Changes Our Table: Nutrition in Midlife and Later Life

When we are young, the question may simply be what we feel like eating. Later, appetite may fade, chewing can become harder and preparing a meal can demand more effort. Yet nutrition becomes no less important with age. Adapting food is a way to preserve strength, choice and the enjoyment of daily life.

We may need less energy, but not less nourishment

Energy needs can decline as activity and body composition change with age. Protein, vitamins and minerals nevertheless remain essential. Eating less is not automatically eating enough—especially with unintended weight loss, frailty or recovery from illness.

Canada's Food Guide emphasizes that healthy eating supports strength, independence and quality of life in older adulthood. Variety, adequate nutrition and meals that people can actually enjoy matter more than an ever-longer list of restrictions.

Muscle needs both food and movement

Muscle tissue changes as we age. Protein supplies building blocks for maintenance and repair, while strength or resistance activity helps preserve function. Neither can replace the other. Eggs, fish, dairy, tofu, beans, nuts and suitable lean meats offer practical options, tailored to preference and chewing ability.

A protein powder is not a longevity formula; equally, living mainly on plain porridge may leave too little protein. With impaired kidney function or a therapeutic diet, protein intake must be individualized by a qualified clinician rather than copied from general fitness advice.

Bone health cannot be bought in one bottle

Strong bones rely on physical activity, nutrition and long-term care. Calcium and vitamin D are important, but fracture risk also depends on strength, vision, home hazards and falls. Dairy or fortified soy drinks, certain tofu products and green vegetables can contribute calcium.

Canadian federal dietary guidance updated in 2026 advises adults aged 51 and older to take a daily supplement of 400 IU (10 micrograms) of vitamin D. Alberta Health Services' existing guidance lists 1,000 IU daily for healthy adults in the same age group. Because these official recommendations differ, check current local advice and your personal circumstances with a clinician or pharmacist rather than assuming more is always better.

When appetite and chewing change, adapt the meal

Dental problems, ill-fitting dentures, difficulty swallowing, changes in taste or smell, and medication side effects may reduce food intake. Dismissing them as inevitable aging can miss problems that deserve attention.

Cook vegetables until soft if needed; consider eggs, tofu, fish or stewed foods that are easier to chew. Use herbs and spices for flavour rather than relying on extra salt. Frequent choking, pain when swallowing, persistent poor appetite or noticeable weight loss calls for professional assessment.

Fluids, vitamin B12 and medicines deserve attention

Thirst may become less noticeable with age, so it can help to drink appropriately through the day. Water, unsweetened drinks, soup and water-rich produce all count. People with medically prescribed fluid limits, including some heart or kidney conditions, should follow individual instructions.

Vitamin B12 is important for nerves and blood cells; some older adults may absorb it less well. Fish, meat, eggs, dairy and fortified foods can supply it. Long-term restricted diets, anemia, numbness or certain medicines are reasons to ask whether testing or supplementation is appropriate—not to self-prescribe high doses.

Eating well helps protect independence and dignity

Some food problems are not about knowledge: shopping may be difficult, cooking tiring, money tight or meals lonely. When food becomes only a table of nutrient numbers, we lose part of what eating means.

Simple meal preparation, sharing food with others, using affordable frozen or canned vegetables and keeping easy protein foods available can be more useful than expensive supplements. Good care preserves safety and nutrition while respecting each person's tastes and independence.

RUNLU · Today's practice

  • Look back over recent meals: were vegetables, fruit, whole grains and suitable protein foods represented?
  • If an older relative is eating less, ask about chewing, taste, mood, shopping and cooking before assuming it is just age.
  • For vitamin D, note the difference between federal guidance (400 IU) and Alberta guidance (1,000 IU) for adults 51+, and confirm an appropriate plan with a health professional.

General health education, not an individualized prescription. Kidney disease, heart failure, swallowing problems, unexplained weight loss and other medical issues require tailored advice.
