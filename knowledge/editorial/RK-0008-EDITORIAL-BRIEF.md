## Reader's practical problem

A small cleaning, flooring or property-service business quotes a fixed price, believes the job has a comfortable margin, then discovers the actual cost exceeded the quote. The article should show how to estimate **job-level direct costs, overhead allocation, scope changes, and contingencies** without conflating **gross margin, markup, net profit, and cash flow**.

This article must be distinct from RK-0007, which addressed revenue, profit and cash timing. RK-0008 addresses **estimate versus actual job profitability**.

## Original fictional teaching example — Canadian dollars

A hypothetical weekly service contract is quoted at **CA$1,200** per month.

| Estimated monthly job costs | CA$ |
|---|---:|
| Direct labour: 24 hours × $25 loaded cost | 600 |
| Supplies | 60 |
| Travel and parking | 40 |
| Allocated overhead | 100 |
| **Estimated total cost** | **800** |
| **Estimated job contribution after allocated overhead** | **400** |

Actual work expands to 34 hours while the fixed quote stays unchanged:

| Actual monthly job costs | CA$ |
|---|---:|
| Direct labour: 34 hours × $25 loaded cost | 850 |
| Supplies | 90 |
| Travel and parking | 60 |
| Allocated overhead | 100 |
| **Actual total cost** | **1,100** |
| **Actual job contribution after allocated overhead** | **100** |

**Illustration:** $400 estimated result falls to $100 actual, a $300 erosion. It does **not** become a loss in this version. To teach an actual loss, introduce a **separate, explicitly costed scenario** rather than mislabeling this result. For example, an additional $150 of approved hypothetical rework would yield actual costs $1,250 and a **$50 job loss**. Clearly state that this is an additional scenario, not the baseline.

The $25/hour rate is an **illustrative loaded labour cost**, not a claim about actual Canadian wages, statutory employer costs, or minimum-wage compliance. Do not infer tax, insurance, benefits or legal requirements from the hypothetical figure.

## Editorial outline

1. The quote looks profitable — what was actually counted?
2. Estimate versus actual: a transparent cost table.
3. Five ways the margin disappears: uncounted hours, scope creep, supplies, travel, rework.
4. Margin versus markup — simple, correctly denominated formulas.
5. Job costing and overhead: explain allocation assumptions, avoid double counting.
6. Change-order and service-scope documentation: practical process, not legal advice.
7. A reusable one-page estimate-versus-actual checklist.
8. When to reprice, renegotiate scope, or decline a job.
9. Educational disclaimer and verified source links.
