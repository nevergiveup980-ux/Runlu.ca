# Why Your Business Can Be Busy but Still Run Out of Cash

**RUNLU KNOWLEDGE · RK-0007 · English original · Editorial draft v0.1 · 2026-11-18**

*This article is for general education. Every business example and amount below is fictional. It is not accounting, tax, lending, or legal advice.*

## The calendar is full. The bank account is not.

Imagine a small commercial-cleaning company. Its owner has just added two regular clients. The team is working more hours than ever. Invoices are going out, and the owner has calculated that the work should earn a profit. Yet Friday arrives, payroll is due, and there is not enough cash in the business account.

How can a business be busy, and even profitable, but short of money?

Because **work performed, profit earned, and cash received are different events**. A sale may be recorded before a customer pays. A supplier may need payment before that customer's money arrives. A full schedule does not change the dates on those obligations.

## Four numbers that tell four different stories

**Revenue** is the value of sales or services recognized for a period under the accounting method used. An invoice is evidence of a claim to payment, not proof that cash has arrived.

**Profit** is revenue less the costs and expenses recognized for that period. Depending on the accounting method, the timing of recognition can differ from the timing of payments. Profit is not a synonym for the bank balance.

**Cash collected** is money actually received. An unpaid invoice is normally a receivable, not spendable cash.

**Cash available** is the money the business can use while still meeting near-term obligations. A bank balance of $8,000 is not necessarily $8,000 of discretionary spending if payroll, supplier invoices, debt payments or required remittances are approaching.

These distinctions matter because a business can report positive profit while temporarily experiencing negative cash flow. The Business Development Bank of Canada explains this difference in its cash-flow guidance [1, 2].

## A profitable month with a cash gap

Consider a **hypothetical** cleaning company with the following simple November activity, in Canadian dollars. To keep the illustration understandable, assume an accrual-style operating profit calculation, no depreciation, no interest, no owner draws, no sales taxes, no income-tax provision, and no other transactions. This is a teaching example, not a complete financial statement.

| November work and operating costs | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Services performed and invoiced | $12,000 |
| Wages attributable to that work | ($6,000) |
| Supplies and other operating expenses | ($2,000) |
| **Illustrative operating profit** | **$4,000** |

The owner can reasonably say the month's work generated an illustrative operating profit of $4,000. But the customer is scheduled to pay the $12,000 invoice on **December 15**. The $8,000 of wages and expenses must be paid in November. Suppose the business starts November with $5,000 cash and has no other cash receipts.

November's cash position becomes **$5,000 + $0 − $8,000 = −$3,000**. The business faces a $3,000 funding gap before the customer pays, despite the $4,000 illustrative profit.

That negative figure is a *projected shortfall*, not a claim that a bank account can freely go below zero. Without an arranged facility or another source of funds, the business would need to change the timing of payments or collections, obtain appropriate financing, reduce spending, or take other lawful steps before bills fall due. A profitable invoice is not itself cash.

## The timing trap is often ordinary

The cash gap may have no dramatic cause. A customer pays in 30 or 45 days. Staff are paid every two weeks. Supplies are purchased before the job begins. Rent is due on the first day of the month. A new contract adds revenue, but also requires more labour before the first payment arrives.

**Accounts receivable** tracks amounts customers owe. **Accounts payable** tracks amounts the business owes suppliers. Both matter, but neither is the same as cash on hand. Canada's federal business-planning guidance specifically warns that credit sales belong in a cash forecast when the cash is expected to be collected, not merely when the sale occurs [3].

Growth can therefore increase the need for working cash. It is possible to win more work and become *less* comfortable in the short term if collections lag behind new expenses.

## A cash calendar beats a hopeful guess

Start with a simple rolling forecast, updated at least weekly when cash is tight:

**Projected closing cash = opening cash + cash expected to arrive − cash expected to leave.**

Use dates, not just monthly totals. For the same fictional business, consider a simplified four-week plan beginning with $5,000. This is a separate timing illustration; its figures are not intended to reproduce the November income statement above.

| Week | Opening cash | Expected receipts | Scheduled payments | Projected closing cash |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $5,000 | $1,000 | $2,000 | $4,000 |
| 2 | $4,000 | $0 | $3,500 | $500 |
| 3 | $500 | $4,000 | $2,500 | $2,000 |
| 4 | $2,000 | $3,000 | $2,000 | $3,000 |

The lowest projected balance is only $500 in week 2. That is the warning signal a monthly revenue total might hide. If the week-3 customer payment slips, the owner needs to revisit the forecast immediately. Expected receipts are estimates, not guaranteed money; a cautious version of the plan should test what happens when important invoices arrive late.

BDC recommends rolling cash-flow forecasting and discusses a 13-week view as one useful management approach [4]. A one-page calendar is a reasonable starting point for a very small business; the discipline matters more than sophisticated software.

## Seven practical habits

1. **Invoice promptly and accurately.** A completed job that has not been billed cannot begin the normal payment process.
2. **Know the actual payment terms.** “Net 30” and “the customer usually pays in 30 days” are not identical assurances. Track observed payment behaviour.
3. **Review receivables every week.** Identify overdue invoices, missing approvals and disputed amounts early. Follow up professionally and keep records.
4. **Put all predictable outflows on the calendar.** Include payroll, supplier bills, rent, loan payments, equipment expenses and applicable tax obligations. Confirm the relevant rules with a qualified adviser.
5. **Build a buffer when feasible.** A target reserve should reflect the company's obligations and variability, not a universal number of weeks.
6. **Stress-test the plan.** Ask what happens if the largest customer pays two weeks late or a repair bill arrives unexpectedly.
7. **Separate the question of timing from the question of margin.** Borrowing to cover a temporary collection gap is a different decision from borrowing to sustain work that consistently loses money.

## Busy does not always mean profitable, either

Cash-flow pressure can hide two different problems. In the first, jobs have healthy margins but customers pay after bills come due. In the second, the business underprices its work or overlooks real costs, so each additional job adds little or no economic benefit.

A cash calendar helps identify *when* money is needed. A properly prepared profit-and-loss analysis helps determine *whether* the work is worth doing. Both questions deserve answers before taking on a large new contract.

## A five-minute owner checklist

Before promising another job, ask: What cash will this job require before the first customer payment? When is that payment realistically expected? Which bills fall due in between? What is the lowest projected bank balance? What happens if the payment is late? And does the quoted price still leave an acceptable margin after all relevant costs?

The lesson is not that growth is dangerous. It is that **a busy schedule describes demand, a profit figure describes performance under an accounting method, and a cash forecast describes whether the business can meet its obligations on time**. A resilient business pays attention to all three.
