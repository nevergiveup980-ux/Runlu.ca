# Why a Profitable Quote Can Turn Into a Losing Job

**RUNLU KNOWLEDGE™ · RK-0008 · English editorial draft v0.1**

**Status:** Internal editorial draft — not published or approved.

A business owner sends a quote for a cleaning contract. The customer accepts. The calendar fills up. The work gets done. Yet at the end of the month, the owner looks at the hours, supply receipts and travel costs and wonders where the expected profit went.

The mistake may not have been the selling price alone. It may have been the difference between **the job that was priced** and **the job that was actually delivered**.

A quote is a prediction. Job costing is the discipline of checking that prediction against what happened.

## 1. What did the original quote actually include?

Consider a fictional small cleaning business in Canada. It agrees to service an office every week for a fixed **CA$1,200 per month**. The owner expects the job to require 24 hours of labour per month.

For this illustration, the owner uses **$25 per hour as an assumed fully loaded labour cost**. That is an invented planning figure, not a statement about local wages, payroll requirements or what any particular worker must be paid.

| Estimated monthly job cost | CA$ |
|---|---:|
| Labour: 24 hours × $25 | 600 |
| Cleaning supplies | 60 |
| Travel and parking | 40 |
| Allocated business overhead | 100 |
| **Estimated total cost** | **800** |
| **Quoted monthly revenue** | **1,200** |
| **Estimated result after these allocated costs** | **400** |

At first glance, the quote appears attractive. Under these assumptions, the job is expected to leave $400 after the costs shown. But that figure is **not necessarily the business's formal net income**. It is a simplified job-level result, dependent on which costs were included and how overhead was assigned.

The quote only works as expected if the time, materials, travel and service scope remain close to the original assumptions.

## 2. What happened after the work began?

In this fictional example, the customer asks for a little more attention to shared areas. Staff take longer than expected to finish the work. Supplies are consumed faster, and travel costs rise. Nobody changes the fixed monthly price.

The actual monthly costs become:

| Actual monthly job cost | CA$ |
|---|---:|
| Labour: 34 hours × $25 | 850 |
| Cleaning supplies | 90 |
| Travel and parking | 60 |
| Allocated business overhead | 100 |
| **Actual total cost** | **1,100** |
| **Monthly revenue, unchanged** | **1,200** |
| **Actual result after these allocated costs** | **100** |

The job has **not lost money** under this baseline scenario. It still shows a positive $100 result after the listed costs. But the expected $400 has fallen to $100: **$300 of the anticipated result has disappeared**.

Where did the $300 go? Ten additional labour hours cost $250; supplies rose by $30; and travel rose by $20. Those changes add up to $300. The allocated overhead remained unchanged in this example.

This is why a busy calendar does not, by itself, demonstrate that individual jobs are meeting their quoted margins.

## 3. A separate scenario: the same job turns into a loss

Now imagine an **additional, separate hypothetical event**: a cleaning error requires corrective work that adds **$150** to the costs above. Assume the business cannot bill that amount to the customer.

The revised total cost is **$1,100 + $150 = $1,250**. With revenue still at $1,200, the result after the costs shown is **negative $50**.

This is the loss scenario. It must not be confused with the baseline example, where the job still produced $100. A $50 loss here is an **illustrative job-level loss after allocated costs**, not a verified statement about a real company's accounting profit or tax result.

The purpose is not to predict that rework will occur. It is to show how little room remains for unexpected costs when the original margin has already narrowed.

## 4. Five ways a quote can quietly deteriorate

**Unrecorded labour.** A task expected to take two hours repeatedly takes nearly three. A few minutes spent unlocking, setting up, collecting supplies or completing paperwork can add up across a month. Record the time actually attributable to the job rather than relying on memory.

**Scope creep.** The customer asks for extra rooms, more frequent service, a new cleaning standard or work that was never part of the original agreement. Sometimes a business chooses to include a small courtesy. The important distinction is between an intentional courtesy and a permanent expansion nobody priced.

**Supplies and equipment.** Chemicals, consumables, replacement parts, wear and disposal may cost more than estimated. Equipment costs require a consistent treatment; do not charge the same item once as a direct cost and again through overhead.

**Travel and access.** Distance, parking, building access, waiting time and return trips can make a short job expensive to service. The quote should reflect the actual operating pattern, not merely the minutes spent inside the customer's premises.

**Rework.** Correcting errors or returning for missed work uses labour and materials that were not in the plan. Track rework separately so it is not mistaken for normal service time.

None of these items automatically justifies charging a customer more. Whether a price can change depends on the agreement, the documented scope and what the parties authorize.

## 5. Margin is not markup

Two percentages can describe the same quote and still mean different things.

Using the **original estimated costs**, the job has revenue of $1,200 and an estimated result of $400 after the costs listed.

**Estimated margin on revenue** = $400 ÷ $1,200 × 100 = **33.3%** (rounded).

**Estimated markup on estimated cost** = $400 ÷ $800 × 100 = **50%**.

These percentages are not interchangeable. Margin uses the selling price as the denominator; markup uses cost. In this article, the $400 figure includes an assumed overhead allocation, so the 33.3% is a **simplified job margin after allocated costs**, not a universally defined accounting gross-profit margin.

Using the **actual baseline costs**, the result is $100 on $1,200 revenue, or about **8.3%** on revenue. After the separate $150 rework scenario, the result is −$50, or approximately **−4.2%** on revenue.

Always name both the numerator and denominator when discussing percentages.

## 6. How to handle overhead without double counting

Direct costs can often be linked to a particular job: labour hours, supplies used on site, a parking receipt or a subcontractor invoice. Overhead may include shared expenses such as administration, bookkeeping, software or office costs.

To compare jobs consistently, a business may allocate a portion of shared overhead to each job using a documented method. The **$100 overhead figure in our example is an assumption**, not a recommended rate. A different allocation method could produce a different job-level result.

Do not confuse an allocated cost with an additional cash payment made that month. Do not add the same vehicle, equipment or administrative expense twice. If the allocation method changes, explain that before comparing one month's job margin with another's.

Job costing also differs from cash-flow management. A job can be profitable under the assumptions used here and still create a cash shortage while the customer has not yet paid. RK-0007 explores that separate timing problem.

## 7. Define the job before the work begins

A useful written scope can be short and plain. It should identify the location, service frequency, rooms or areas included, expected tasks, special requests, access arrangements, who supplies consumables, and the agreed price.

For recurring service, distinguish **regular tasks** from **periodic or extra tasks**. For example, weekly office cleaning may not automatically include carpet extraction, window washing or post-renovation cleanup.

When a request changes, record what changed, the expected additional time or cost, and whether the customer has authorized a revised price or scope. The aim is to prevent surprises, not to turn every small conversation into a dispute.

Keep the customer's actual agreement and applicable rules in view; this article is not legal advice and does not establish a right to unilaterally alter a contract.

## 8. A one-page estimate-versus-actual checklist

Use the following fields for each job or recurring contract. A notebook, spreadsheet or simple digital tool can work.

| Checkpoint | Before accepting | After the work |
|---|---|---|
| Agreed price | Record quoted price and billing period | Confirm actual billable amount |
| Scope | List included areas and tasks | Record additions or exclusions |
| Labour | Estimate hours and assumed loaded cost | Record actual hours and comparable cost |
| Supplies | Estimate consumables | Record actual consumption |
| Travel | Estimate visits, distance, parking | Record actual visits and costs |
| Equipment | Choose direct charge or allocation method | Apply the same method consistently |
| Overhead | Document allocation assumption | Reconcile consistently |
| Rework | Note potential risk and contingency | Track actual corrective work separately |
| Changes | Define who can approve extras | Save approvals and revised terms |
| Result | Revenue minus included estimated costs | Revenue minus comparable actual costs |

For a recurring job, review the results after the first few service visits, not only at the end of the year. Compare the same period and the same categories of cost. If the job requires more time than quoted, determine whether the cause is temporary, operational or a lasting scope change.

## 9. When should the owner change course?

**Improve the process** when the scope is stable but the team is losing time through avoidable travel, setup or inefficient scheduling. Efficiency should not come at the expense of safety or the agreed service quality.

**Discuss scope** when the customer regularly requests work outside the agreement. Document the additional work and offer a clear revised arrangement rather than allowing unpriced extras to become the default.

**Revisit pricing** when comparable actual costs consistently exceed the assumptions behind the quote. Explain the reason and respect existing contractual commitments.

**Decline or stop pursuing a job** when the available price cannot support the work under reasonable assumptions and the scope cannot be changed. More revenue is not always better revenue.

The central question is not simply, “Did we win the contract?” It is, **“Did we understand what it would cost to deliver what we promised?”**

## 10. The habit that protects a small business

The most useful job-costing system may be a simple routine: **estimate, record, compare, investigate, adjust**.

An estimate gives a reasoned starting point. Actual records show where that starting point was wrong. A comparison turns an uncomfortable surprise into something the business can learn from.

A full calendar is evidence of demand. A signed quote is evidence of an agreement. Neither is proof of job-level profitability. That proof requires attention to the work performed, the costs incurred and the assumptions used to measure the result.
