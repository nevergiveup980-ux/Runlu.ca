Welcome to the AGI Era

After the Threshold · English publication opening

The text below is from the published English edition.

September 3, 2026. 7:17 p.m.

It had just rained in Calder.

Chen Mo stood at the kitchen sink, turning the last bowl upside down on the drying rack. The television in the living room was off, but his phone was propped against the spice jars, playing a technology launch he had never intended to watch closely.

He could not have said why he had not swiped it away.

Maybe it was the title, oversized and impossible to ignore: GPT-6 Astra.

Maybe it was the man on the screen explaining that the model did more than answer questions now. It could operate a computer. It could move between a browser, a spreadsheet, software, and websites and complete tasks on its own.

Chen dried his hands on a towel.

“So it can work now,” he murmured.

His wife called from upstairs. “What?”

“Nothing.”

Near the end of the livestream, the speaker paused.

The sentence that followed would be replayed on news programs, podcasts, in classrooms, and before parliamentary committees for years.

Welcome to the AGI era.

Chen did not know then that the words had been spoken by OpenAI president Greg Brockman. He did not know that, beginning the next morning, those seven English words would be clipped into a dozen-second sound bite and played again and again. Some would call it a historic declaration. Others would dismiss it as marketing. Still others would argue seriously over whether AGI could ever have a single moment of arrival that anyone had the authority to announce.

What Chen noticed was the comment stream, moving too fast to read. People wanted to know when they could use it. People wanted to know whether their companies would start laying people off. Someone insisted that real AGI could not be a model that still made mistakes. Someone else had already posted a list of “The Ten Skills You Must Learn This Year.” Beneath one livestream, the future seemed to have opened dozens of doors, all leading in contradictory directions.

Elsewhere, the head of a small company deleted the next day’s recruiting event from his calendar. A graduate student changed the title of a thesis three months in the making to “Responsibility Boundaries for Autonomous Agents.” A driver coming off the night shift saw a headline about AGI at a gas station and flicked past it without reading. One of the loudest moments in the history of technology entered most lives without making a sound.

Years later, Chen would remember that. The things that truly change a life rarely do so at the moment they are announced. They do it months later, the first time they perform some task you had assumed would always belong to you.

At the time, Chen felt nothing unusual.

He still had to open the warehouse at 6:45 the next morning. Three trucks were due shortly after seven. Twelve rolls of carpet had to be unloaded. Two stores had transfers waiting. One supplier had an irritating habit of putting the wrong labels on two products whose colours were almost identical.

No matter how intelligent an AI became, it was not going to roll twelve carpets off a trailer for him.

At least that was what he thought that night.

Across town, twenty-three-year-old Maya Bennett was texting her mother.

They say the AGI era is here.

Her mother sent back a smiling face and then: Isn’t that perfect? You studied data analytics. You’re entering the field at exactly the right time.

Maya stared at the message and thought it made perfect sense.

She had graduated four months earlier and landed a job at the consulting firm she had wanted since sophomore year. Her manager had told her that the first two years were about paying dues: research, cleaning data, making charts, revising slides, turning a senior person’s thoughts into language a client could understand.

Maya was not afraid of hard work.

She had almost been looking forward to it.

Outside her apartment, a rideshare car pulled to the curb. Its driver, Luis Ortega, looked down at his next pickup. He caught a radio host saying something about “AGI” and switched the station to a hockey game.

Ten minutes later he spent twenty-seven minutes crawling along Airport Road.

One driver cut in. Another braked for no reason. Someone missed an exit and lurched across three lanes at once.

Luis watched the red taillights and laughed.

“Let the robots try this.”

At Calder Central Hospital, Dr. Helen Ward had already been on shift for nine hours.

She had not watched the launch.

At 1:00 a.m., a forty-nine-year-old man arrived in the emergency department with chest pain. His first ECG showed nothing classic. His blood pressure was not especially alarming. The hospital’s recently deployed clinical support system placed a small yellow box in the lower-right corner of the screen: Consider additional assessment for aortic dissection risk.

Helen frowned.

The patient did not sound like a dissection.

She closed the alert.

Ten seconds later, she opened it again.

At 1:27, she ordered additional imaging.

At 2:04, the result came back.

The system was right.

Helen stood in front of the monitor without moving for a long time.

A nurse assumed she was relieved.

She was.

But for the first time, Helen realized that what had frightened her most was not the thought that the patient might die.

It was the possibility that the small yellow box had seen something she had not.

After three in the morning, clips from the launch began appearing in different lengths on different screens. Some people reposted only “the AGI era.” Some shared the browser-control demo. Some set it beside decade-old headlines promising that self-driving cars were two years away and laughed. The excitement was a dusting of snow over ordinary life. The change underneath had not yet acquired weight, which meant everyone was still free to believe in it or not.

Before bed, Chen checked his phone one last time. No one in the warehouse group chat was talking about AGI. A night-shift employee had posted a photo of a damaged roll and asked what to do with it in the morning.

Chen replied: Don’t receive it yet.

After he sent the message, he found himself thinking that this was reality: a model worth billions of dollars had just been unveiled, and the first problem waiting for him tomorrow was still a roll of carpet whose plastic wrap had been punctured by a forklift.

Maya could not sleep. She opened the company’s internal learning portal. A new course had appeared on the front page: From Using AI to Managing AI. Runtime: forty-seven minutes. She watched eight, then closed it.

She could not name the feeling that had made her restless. It was not fear exactly. It was more like stepping onto a staircase and realizing that, while you were climbing, someone had quietly changed the direction in which it led.

Helen left the hospital at four in the morning. The parking lot was nearly empty. She sat in her car without starting it and searched the condition the clinical system had just flagged. She knew she was not studying. She was trying to answer a different question: Without that yellow alert, at what point would I have thought of it?

She scrolled through page after page, then finally turned the phone facedown on the passenger seat.

There was no answer.

The next morning, everyone woke up as usual.

Coffee machines hissed.

Traffic lights changed.

Schools took attendance.

Warehouse doors opened.

There was no bell, no siren, no official announcement of the minute the old world had begun to end.

Only later did people understand that a threshold is rarely a doorway.

It is more like rising water.

By the time you notice your shoes are wet, the river has already reached you.
