CHAPTER ONE · RUNLU ORIGINAL

An Ordinary Friday

After the Threshold · English publication opening

The text below is from the published English edition.

Friday, September 4.

At 6:43 a.m., Chen Mo pulled into the second parking space on the east side of the warehouse.

He always parked there. It was not company policy. It was habit.

Twenty-three years earlier, when he had taken his first warehouse job, an old supervisor had told him that the person responsible for opening the building should park where everyone could see the car. Drivers arrived, salespeople arrived, installers arrived, and one glance told them someone was inside.

Since then, Chen had worked in three warehouses under two owners and through five generations of software. No manual had ever contained that rule.

He had never forgotten it.

At 6:47 he badged through the door.

The warehouse lights came on row by row, the building stirring like an animal waking from sleep.

He checked the whiteboard at the entrance, then the receiving schedule on his phone. At 7:10, the first supplier truck arrived early.

The driver was new.

“Dock three?” the man asked.

Chen glanced at the height of the trailer.

“Two. The ramp at three is wet.”

The driver looked toward Dock Three. There was only a thin sheen of water, barely visible.

“My system says three.”

“Your system didn’t get yesterday afternoon’s rain.”

The driver shrugged and backed toward Dock Two.

At 7:26, the first roll came off the trailer. The label read Stone Grey. Chen caught one glimpse of the colour showing at the edge of the wrap and raised a hand.

“Hold it.”

“What’s wrong?”

“That isn’t Stone Grey.”

Jason, one of the warehouse staff, brought the scanner close. “Barcode’s right.”

Chen crouched and pulled the plastic back with two fingers.

“Barcode’s right. Product’s wrong. This is Fog Ash.”

Jason grinned. “Your eyes beat the database.”

“Not my eyes.” Chen stood. “They made the same labeling mistake last month.”

“Even better. The database doesn’t hold grudges.”

They both laughed.

At 9:17, an email arrived from the director of operations. Subject: Astra Readiness Working Group.

Chen did not open it immediately.

At 10:30, sitting in the small office and eating a banana, he finally clicked it.

The email was brief. Corporate headquarters planned to assess next-generation AI agents for purchasing, inventory, customer service, quotations, and internal administration. Department heads were to submit, within two weeks, a list of tasks that could be automated.

Chen swallowed the banana and read that phrase again.

Tasks that could be automated.

His first reaction was relief.

There was so much repetition in every day: checking inventory, answering email, confirming orders, finding shipping documents, explaining how much of a product remained and which store needed what. If an AI could remove half of it, he could stop working so many late days.

He took out a sheet of paper and began writing.

Inventory lookup.

Generate transfer orders.

Verify receiving quantities.

Low-stock alerts.

Customer pickup status.

At the fifth item, he stopped.

The receiving slip from that morning was still on the desk.

The system knew the barcode.

He knew the supplier’s habits.

Until now those facts had never been in conflict, because one belonged to a tool and the other to a person.

But what happened when the tool learned every mistake?

What if next month it, too, knew that Fog Ash was often mislabeled as Stone Grey?

What if it did not merely know, but knew before he did?

Chen turned the paper facedown.

At eleven, Kevin from sales came looking for a roll the system showed as unavailable.

“We have it,” Chen said.

“Where?”

“Back row of 5C. Second roll from the bottom.”

Kevin stared at him. “How do you know?”

“Someone returned it yesterday and scanned it into the wrong location.”

They walked to 5C together.

The roll was there.

Kevin took a picture with his phone and joked, “The company should download your brain.”

Chen laughed and swore at him.

After Kevin left, Chen remained beside the roll for a few extra seconds.

Download your brain.

At lunch, Chen showed his automation list to Jason. Jason immediately added seven more items: supplier ETA alerts, cutting-ticket checks, store follow-ups, return-photo filing, forklift maintenance reminders. By the time he finished, he was laughing.

“At this rate, we’ll all go home early.”

Chen said, “And what do we do with the time we save?”

Jason blinked. “More important work.”

“What’s more important?”

Jason opened his mouth, then grinned. “The boss will tell us.”

The joke stayed with Chen longer than it should have. In the past, efficiency had always meant the same people could do more. But if efficiency became fast enough to remove whole blocks of work, “time saved” stopped being merely a benefit. It became blank space in which a person might have to explain his value again.

At two in the afternoon, a new warehouse employee steered a roll into the wrong aisle. Chen did not correct him immediately. Instead, he made him sit on the forklift and study the turn twice. On the second attempt, the young man noticed that if he followed the system’s shortest route, the roll core would clip a fire-protection column.

Chen said, “There are two maps in a warehouse. One is on the computer. One is in your head. The second one isn’t there so you can argue with the first. It’s there so you know when the first one forgot to draw a column.”

The words silenced Chen himself.

Because the email from corporate was, in its own way, an attempt to put that second map into the computer too.

At 4:38, the warehouse quieted.

Before turning off the lights, Chen looked once more at the morning email.

He was not afraid.

More precisely, he did not yet have a reason to be afraid.

For the first time, though, he found himself thinking about something he had never considered before.

If a company could one day “download” a person’s experience completely, what difference would it make whether that person showed up the next morning?
