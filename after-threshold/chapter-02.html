CHAPTER TWO · RUNLU ORIGINAL

The One Who Never Asks for a Raise

After the Threshold · English publication opening

The text below is from the published English edition.

Maya Bennett first met the Nova agent in a windowless conference room.

October 12. Ten in the morning.

The project manager dimmed the lights and projected his laptop onto the wall.

“Don’t think of it as a chatbot,” he said. “And don’t think of it as a search engine. Think of it as a junior project team that never goes offline.”

There were seven people in the room.

Four of them, Maya included, had graduated less than two years earlier.

The manager demonstrated a task that normally took them two days: collect public information on five competing companies, organize six quarters of key metrics, normalize definitions, create trend charts, identify anomalies, and produce a ten-page internal brief.

Nova asked three clarifying questions.

Eleven minutes later, the first draft appeared.

No one spoke.

Maya noticed the mistake first.

For one company, the margin in a quarter had been misread because a PDF footnote described a change in accounting treatment.

She nearly felt relieved.

“This is wrong,” she said quickly.

The manager nodded. “Exactly. Which is why we still need you.”

The sentence was meant to reassure them.

Maya heard something else.

Still.

We still need you.

That afternoon, she spent forty minutes finding three more problems: a currency conversion, a post-acquisition reporting change, and a one-time gain that the company had folded into adjusted earnings.

A year earlier, those catches would have made her proud.

Now she felt like the person assigned to inspect the last five marks on a test after a machine had already scored ninety-five.

At seven that evening, the office had emptied except for Maya and Aiden, another analyst from her hiring class.

Aiden pushed aside his takeout container.

“Do you think they’ll lay us off?”

“Not that fast,” Maya said, still looking at her screen.

“I don’t either.”

Neither of them asked, What about later?

On Friday, the company sent an internal notice: next year’s campus hiring target would fall from sixty-four to twenty-eight.

The explanation was beautifully written.

Organizational optimization.

Higher-leverage human-AI collaboration.

Concentrating talent investment in high-judgment roles.

Maya read the email from top to bottom.

She remembered the career fair in her senior year: long lines, carefully printed resumes, students wearing suits that did not quite fit, rehearsing introductions in restroom mirrors.

Everyone had been competing for those sixty-four seats.

No one knew that thirty-six of them would eventually lose not to better graduates, but to something that would never attend a career fair at all.

The following week, Nova entered the actual project workflow. A morning update that used to rotate among four junior analysts now ran automatically at six. By the time Maya arrived, trend charts were drawn, anomalies were highlighted, and likely client questions had already been listed. Her job had shifted from making the work to deciding what could not safely be sent.

During a one-on-one, her manager praised her.

“You catch model errors quickly. That’s going to be an important skill.”

“How long?” Maya asked.

His pen stopped.

“What do you mean?”

“What if the next version fixes those errors too?”

He smiled the way someone might smile at a twenty-three-year-old worrying about retirement benefits.

“Then you move up to higher-level judgment.”

“What judgment?”

“What the client is actually trying to solve. Which risks are worth taking. How to persuade people.”

Maya nodded. She knew it was the correct answer. She also heard the hidden requirement inside it.

The firm used to hire sixty-four young people every year and let them acquire judgment through thousands of low-level tasks. If the low-level tasks vanished first, where were new people supposed to obtain the experience required to become “high-judgment talent”?

That night Aiden sent her an old project time sheet. Two years earlier, comparable work had required nine analysts, three managers, and a partner. The new budget showed three analysts.

Aiden wrote: We’re not being laid off. We’re the people who won’t get hired in the first place.

Maya stared at the message and did not reply.

She thought about the thirty-six campus positions that had disappeared. Those students would never receive a termination notice. On some evening in spring, they would receive a courteous sentence instead: Thank you for your interest.

Before the next campus recruiting season, Maya was assigned to an online information session. More than four hundred student names appeared on the screen. They asked about visas, rotations, and whether the firm still offered a path from entry-level analyst to project manager. The host passed the last question to Maya.

For the first time, she did not know how to be honest without being cruel, or reassuring without being false.

The company did still train young employees. There were simply fewer of them. Experience that had once accumulated through a hundred spreadsheets, dozens of client meetings, and a long series of low-stakes mistakes was now being skipped by agents. New hires were expected on day one to exercise judgment that used to belong to year three.

At last Maya said, “We care more and more about whether you can explain why you would reject an answer that looks very good.”

Someone in the chat immediately asked: How are we supposed to prove that? If companies don’t give us the first job, where do we learn to reject a good answer?

The host moved on quickly.

Maya copied the question into her notebook after the session.

Education and employers, she realized, were both beginning to demand experience from young people while simultaneously shrinking the low-risk spaces in which experience was created. Technology might be taking more than jobs. It might also be taking something the old world had quietly assumed every beginner would receive: permission to be clumsy for three years.

That weekend, Maya ate dinner at her parents’ house.

“How’s work?” her mother asked.

“Good.”

“Didn’t you say you’re using that new AI?”

“Yeah.”

“Must make things easier.”

Maya picked up a piece of roasted potato and said nothing.

Her father laughed. “Every new technology comes with people saying all the jobs will disappear. They said the same thing when your grandfather’s factory brought in robots. People still had jobs.”

Maya nodded.

Her father was not wrong.

That was precisely what made the thought so difficult.

The world might never run out of work.

But it could very easily run out of the kind of work she had spent years preparing to begin with.

Before bed, she opened her university’s website.

The homepage showed smiling first-year students beneath the line:

Build the skills for tomorrow.

Maya stared at it for a long time.

She found herself wondering what gave a university the right to claim it knew what tomorrow would need.
