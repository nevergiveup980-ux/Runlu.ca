CHAPTER THREE · RUNLU ORIGINAL

The Car Without a Driver

After the Threshold · English publication opening

The text below is from the published English edition.

On November 23, the Calder City Council approved a pilot autonomous-driving ordinance by a vote of nine to six.

Luis Ortega heard about it at a gas station.

A taxi driver at the next pump swore under his breath.

Luis did not.

He replaced the nozzle and looked at the total on the screen, mentally calculating how much more he needed that week to cover the mortgage, car payment, insurance, and his daughter’s dance classes.

That evening, he opened a driver forum. The most popular thread was not about safety. It was about what their vehicles were still worth.

Someone had just paid sixty thousand dollars for a seven-passenger SUV with five years left on the loan. Someone else owned two commercial taxi plates he had planned to sell when he retired. Another driver wanted to know whether, as autonomous accident rates kept falling, insurance for human commercial drivers would rise instead.

For the first time, Luis understood that a technology entering a city did not merely replace a job. It traveled down through the assets, loans, insurance policies, repair shops, parking lots, and family plans built around that job. What a driver might lose was not eight hours a day behind a steering wheel. It was an entire economic life built over decades around the fact that he knew how to drive.

One veteran driver posted: I’m not afraid of a machine driving better than me. I’m afraid the plate that was worth $100,000 last year will be a souvenir next year.

Luis did not reply.

He looked down at his car key. The metal edge had been polished bright by seven years in his hand. It was also something he still owed the bank money for.

For the first time, a technology headline had stepped out of the screen and taken a seat beside his bills.

His hybrid had eighteen months left on a five-year loan.

The city said the first phase would contain only sixty robotaxis, limited to downtown, the airport corridor, and the university district. They would pause in severe weather.

Sixty.

The number seemed almost laughably small.

Calder had so many cars.

What could sixty do?

Three weeks later, he saw one in real traffic for the first time.

It was 12:40 a.m. He had dropped off a passenger whose flight had been delayed and was driving south from the airport.

At a red light, a silver-grey SUV stopped to his left.

The sensor rig on the roof was obvious.

The driver’s seat was empty.

A woman in her twenties slept against the rear window.

Luis stared for several seconds.

The light turned green.

The SUV neither lunged nor hesitated. It moved off smoothly.

At the next intersection, a drunk pedestrian suddenly stepped over the median.

Luis’s foot was already on the brake when the silver SUV slowed almost simultaneously, controlling the distance more gently than he did.

The pedestrian stumbled across.

The driverless car continued.

Luis followed it for another block and, for the first time, did not feel like laughing.

Two days later, he picked up a passenger in the university district headed for the airport.

The man, a software engineer, was on the phone as soon as he got in.

“No, the testing roles aren’t all gone. They merged the manual regression team... yeah... they’re keeping three people to review the agent system’s results...”

When the call ended, he exhaled hard.

“Layoffs?” Luis asked in the mirror.

“Not me.”

“That’s good.”

The passenger was silent for a moment. “My wife is on that team.”

Luis did not know what to say.

At the airport, the passenger paid and opened the door, then paused.

“Are you scared of self-driving cars?”

“Scared of what?”

“Losing your job.”

Luis smiled. “You software people worry about yourselves first.”

The passenger laughed too.

Neither laugh sounded natural.

By December, the platform was offering discounts to passengers who chose autonomous cars.

Luis’s evening rides fell about eleven percent.

In January, they were down eighteen.

That same month, his commercial insurance renewal rose nine percent.

The broker explained that claim costs for human rideshare drivers had not declined in step with the autonomous fleet, and the market’s risk pool was changing.

Luis understood almost none of the second half.

He understood fewer rides and higher costs.

At the airport waiting area, the drivers’ conversations changed. They used to talk about gas prices, hockey, and which incoming flight was most likely to produce a long fare. Now they talked about the small grey cars on their screens.

Some said the first real snowstorm would finish them. Some said the city would eventually cap the fleet. Others were already asking used-car dealers whether selling commercial vehicles early would reduce their losses.

A driver named Hassan showed Luis a job posting on his phone. The platform was hiring Autonomous Fleet Field Support. The work included clearing sensors, handling passenger belongings, dealing with road closures, and responding when vehicles could not resolve something on their own. The pay was not spectacular, but it was hourly and came with benefits.

“Didn’t you used to repair cars?” Hassan asked.

Luis pushed the phone back. “I drive because I got tired of crawling under cars.”

“So how long are you going to keep driving?”

“Until they learn to back down an alley in February.”

Hassan laughed. Luis did too.

Back in his car, Luis searched for the posting again. He stayed on the page for two minutes and did not apply.

Admitting that a new job was worth considering felt too much like admitting that the old one might have an ending. People often saw the exit. What they resisted was admitting that they were already on a road that required one.

At the end of the month, Luis rebuilt the family budget. The mortgage could not be cut. Neither could insurance. He left his daughter’s dance lessons untouched. What disappeared instead was the twenty-odd dollars he spent on coffee with friends every Sunday and the set of tires he had planned to replace in spring.

He stared at the spreadsheet for a long time.

“Orders down eighteen percent” was not a number anymore. It had become a handful of small absences in his life, invisible to anyone who did not know why they were gone.

One evening, his daughter Sofia sat at the kitchen table working on university applications.

“Dad, I want to apply to that school in Vancouver.”

“Apply.”

“It costs more than staying here.”

“Apply first.”

“What if I don’t get a scholarship?”

Luis shut the refrigerator door.

“Get accepted. Then we’ll talk.”

Sofia watched him. “Have you been getting fewer rides lately?”

“Who told you that?”

“You never used to be home this early on Fridays.”

Luis looked down at his phone.

The map was quiet.

A few seconds later, a request appeared.

He grabbed his keys.

“See? Busy.”

At the door, he stopped and looked back.

“Apply where you want. Money is not your problem.”

After the door closed, Sofia returned to her laptop.

Luis sat in his car without starting it.

At the exit to the parking lot, a driverless taxi glided past.

There was no passenger in the back.

It was on its way to pick up the next one.

Luis realized that the frightening thing was not that the car knew how to drive.

It was that the car had no daughter whose tuition was due.
