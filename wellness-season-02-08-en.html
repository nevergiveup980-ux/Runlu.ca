Vegetables and Fruit: The Nutrition Science Behind Their Colours

At a market, the first thing we notice is often not a nutrition label but colour: a red tomato, bright green leaves, an orange carrot, purple grapes. Colours are inviting, and they offer a helpful reminder to seek variety. But a colour is a clue—not a guarantee of exceptional health benefits. The foods and habits we repeat matter more.

Colour is a clue, not a nutrition label

Red tomatoes, orange carrots, green leafy vegetables and purple cabbage make a meal inviting. Pigments such as chlorophyll, carotenoids and anthocyanins contribute to these colours and reflect some of the diversity of plants.

But appearance cannot precisely tell us how much fibre, vitamin or mineral a food contains. Variety, ripeness, storage and cooking all matter. Rather than memorizing magical colour rules, aim for a genuinely varied diet.

Green and orange vegetables deserve a regular place

Dark leafy greens, cruciferous vegetables and broccoli can provide folate, vitamin K and other nutrients, with amounts varying among foods. Orange vegetables such as carrots, squash and sweet potatoes contain carotenoids, including beta-carotene, which the body can convert to vitamin A.

Canada's Food Guide encourages dark green vegetables every day and orange vegetables a few times a week. This does not mean other colours are unimportant; these groups simply help supply particular nutrients.

Red, purple and pale-coloured foods also matter

The red colour of tomatoes and watermelon can come partly from lycopene; the purple-blue colour of berries and red cabbage may reflect anthocyanins. Researchers study these plant compounds, but laboratory antioxidant activity does not prove that any particular food prevents or cures disease.

Pale vegetables deserve a place too. Cauliflower, onions, mushrooms and potatoes supply different nutrients. A food is not nutritionally empty because it lacks a bright colour.

Whole fruit and juice are not the same

A whole apple, berries or citrus fruit provides water, naturally occurring sugars, fibre and other nutrients. Juicing can remove some fibre and alter the structure of the original food; juice can also be consumed quickly without the same sense of fullness.

Canada's Food Guide recommends whole or cut vegetables and fruits over juice. Even freshly squeezed juice contains free sugars naturally present in fruit juice; no added sugar does not make juice equivalent to whole fruit.

Fresh, frozen and canned can all fit

Fresh produce is useful, but frozen and suitably canned vegetables and fruits can also be healthy choices. Nutrient levels vary with processing, storage and cooking; 'fresh only' is not a sound rule.

For frozen products, look for options without added sugars or rich sauces. For canned vegetables, compare sodium; for canned fruit, look for little or no added sugar. Budget, season and convenience matter.

Turn colour into a lasting habit

Canada's Food Guide suggests filling roughly half the plate with vegetables and fruits. It is a visual guide, not a demand to include every colour at every meal. What matters is variety and reasonable portions over time.

Healthy eating need not be expensive or built around 'superfoods'. Cabbage, carrots, apples and frozen berries all have a place. Wash produce appropriately, cook it in ways you enjoy and account for personal dietary needs.

RUNLU · Today's practice

  • Recall the types of produce you ate during the last three days; you need not include every colour daily.
  • Next time you shop, add one dark green or orange vegetable you do not eat often.
  • Choose whole fruit over some servings of juice and consider frozen or canned produce when practical.

For general nutrition education only, not individualized medical advice. People who need to manage potassium, take medicines affected by changes in vitamin K intake, or have special dietary restrictions should seek professional guidance.
