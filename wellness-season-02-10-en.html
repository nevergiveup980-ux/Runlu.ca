When We Eat: Meals, Late-Night Snacks and Intermittent Fasting

When should we eat? Is breakfast compulsory? Must we stop eating after nine at night? Meal timing has become another set of health rules. Yet a clock is a useful reminder, not a substitute for what our bodies need. The real challenge is balancing routine, food quality, sleep and real life.

Our bodies have rhythms; eating does too

Sleep, wakefulness, body temperature and many metabolic processes follow circadian rhythms. Meals can also serve as timing cues. But this does not prove that morning calories cannot contribute to weight gain, or that eating at night always causes harm.

Health outcomes depend on total intake, dietary quality, sleep, movement and personal circumstances. Shift workers and caregivers may have very different schedules. A reasonably consistent rhythm is helpful without requiring one universal timetable.

Must we eat exactly three meals?

Breakfast, lunch and dinner are a practical routine for many households. Regular meals can make it easier to plan balanced foods and avoid impulsive snacks when very hungry.

But there is no scientific law that everybody must eat three times a day. Some prefer three meals; others need smaller snacks around work or activity. Nutritional adequacy, overall intake, hunger cues and sustainability matter more than a fixed meal count.

Should we always eat breakfast?

For some, breakfast provides useful energy and an opportunity to eat whole grains, protein foods and fruit. But research does not support absolute claims that breakfast alone determines body weight, metabolism or longevity.

Someone without morning appetite need not force a meal simply to obey a slogan, provided they meet nutritional needs later. People with diabetes, particular glucose-lowering medicines, pregnancy or other health conditions may need individual guidance.

Late-night eating: the issue is not only the clock

A small snack when hungry in the evening differs from routinely eating large, energy-dense meals deep into the night while sleeping too little. Frequent late snacks may raise total daily energy intake. A heavy meal just before bed may worsen reflux or sleep discomfort for some.

People who must eat late because of work need not feel guilty. Planning ahead, moderating portions and adjusting rich meals to personal digestion and bedtime can be more helpful than a rigid curfew.

Intermittent fasting is a method, not a miracle

Intermittent fasting includes daily eating windows, alternate-day approaches and reduced intake on selected days. For some adults it may be a practical way to lower total calories and manage weight.

In a randomized 12-month study involving 139 adults with obesity, eight-hour time-restricted eating did not achieve statistically significantly greater weight loss than calorie restriction alone when both groups restricted calories. The trial does not settle every question about fasting, but it does not justify claims about guaranteed metabolic resets, detoxification or longer life.

Give the body a routine, not a rigid rulebook

Try making time for meals, noticing hunger and fullness, and reducing distracted snacking or chronic sleep loss. Canada's Food Guide encourages awareness of when, why and how we eat.

Children and adolescents, pregnant or breastfeeding people, those with current or past eating disorders, and those taking insulin or drugs that can cause low blood sugar should not undertake strict fasting without appropriate clinical advice. A nourishing, practical routine is often worth more than an impressive eating-window number.

RUNLU · Today's practice

  • Observe meal and sleep times for three days. Are meals missed because of work, or snacks eaten automatically late at night?
  • Plan one meal that is consistently difficult to fit into your schedule; change one habit at a time.
  • Before trying fasting, consider medications, health conditions, work schedules and nutritional needs. Seek professional advice if relevant.

General nutrition education only; not personal medical advice. Strict fasting can be unsafe in certain medical or life circumstances, especially with glucose-lowering medication, pregnancy, breastfeeding, young age, low body weight or eating disorders.
