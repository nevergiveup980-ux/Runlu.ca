## Chapter 5 — Martin Says Don’t Look

Martin called Chen Mo into his office for the second time on Wednesday afternoon.

This time the door was closed. There were no files on the desk, only an old brass stapler. Chen Mo had seen it before. It had belonged to William and was heavy as a small brick.

“Is Laura still looking into it?” Martin asked.

“I didn’t tell her to.”

“But she will.”

“She’s Finance.”

Martin nodded, then asked, “Maya?”

“She hasn’t stopped either.”

“And you?”

Chen Mo did not answer. Martin smiled. “Your biggest problem is that you think not answering counts as an answer.”

Chen Mo said, “Then I’ll be honest. I want to know.”

Martin leaned back. “And after you know?”

“I’ll have to know first before I know that.”

Martin stood and took a thin cardboard box from a cabinet. “My father had an irritating saying: not every correct answer is worth what it costs a company.”

Chen Mo said, “That doesn’t sound like a correct statement.”

“I thought the same thing for years.”

Martin set the box on the desk and began talking about 2001. The company had expanded its warehouse too quickly, several major customers delayed payment, the bank tightened credit, suppliers switched to COD, and payroll nearly failed.

“How many people were laid off?” Chen Mo asked.

“Twelve.”

Martin opened the box. Inside were old notes, photocopies, and fax sheets. The top note read: DON’T CUT ROBERT UNTIL FRIDAY. Beneath it were two initials: E.W.

“Who was Robert?”

“A delivery driver. His daughter was having surgery that week. Four more days on the insurance would cover it.”

“He was still laid off?”

“Yes.”

“So what did the note save?”

Martin looked at it. “Four days. Sometimes four days is everything.”

Chen Mo asked who had written it. Martin said he did not know. His father, William, had never admitted to it either.

“Why do you keep trying to make Ethan a person?” Martin asked suddenly.

Chen Mo looked up.

Martin did not explain. He only said, “A company isn’t a puzzle. It’s a lot of people working together for a long time and leaving behind consequences. Some have names. Some don’t.”

Chen Mo carried the box to a small meeting room and spread more than twenty documents across the table with Maya. They quickly found that the E.W. notes had at least four clearly different handwriting styles.

And E.W. was not always kind. One exceptional release form demanded that responsibility be traced first; another equipment-damage note required identifying the responsible person before repair approval.

Maya said, “That doesn’t sound like the helpful person everyone keeps describing.”

Chen Mo answered, “That’s what makes it sound human.”

When Martin came in and saw the handwriting groups, his expression became complicated. He admitted that during the 2001 crisis, putting Ethan’s name on a decision could make it feel like “the company’s decision” instead of one particular person’s decision.

“So the name also helped management feel less guilty?” Chen Mo asked.

Martin did not deny it immediately. “Maybe. In organizational legends, warmth and convenience are often two sides of the same thing.”

He turned the note over. The back was blank.

“Don’t make Ethan too romantic,” Martin said. “The name helped people, and it also protected the people making decisions.”

Chen Mo picked up the box and headed for the door. Martin spoke behind him. “If you end up discovering Ethan never existed, don’t be disappointed.”

Chen Mo turned. “And if he did?”

Martin looked out the window.

“That could be more trouble.”
