The Lure of Sugar: Blood Glucose, Insulin and Modern Eating

Sweetness brings pleasure, but also concern. Some treat sugar as a universal enemy, while others believe it is harmless unless weight increases. Our bodies are more complex. Understanding glucose, insulin and everyday food helps us choose without fear or excess.

Sugar is not the body's only fuel

Many cells use glucose for energy. The brain and red blood cells have particular needs for a steady supply of glucose. Starch also becomes glucose during digestion, so avoiding sweets does not mean that no sugar enters the blood.

The body can use other sources of energy, including fat. Carbohydrates need not be treated as enemies, and an increase in blood glucose after eating is not automatically a sign of illness.

What does insulin actually do?

After a meal, blood glucose commonly rises. Insulin, released by the pancreas, helps tissues such as muscle and fat take up or store glucose. Other hormones, including glucagon, also help regulate blood sugar.

Insulin is not a harmful substance to be eliminated. More relevant concerns include impaired long-term glucose regulation and insulin resistance. Diabetes cannot be diagnosed from a single feeling after eating or one home meter reading.

Intrinsic, added and free sugars

A whole apple and a sugary beverage may both contain sugar, but their nutritional contexts differ. Whole fruit provides fibre, water and other nutrients, and can affect fullness and eating pace differently.

The WHO defines free sugars as sugars added to foods or drinks plus sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juice and juice concentrates. It does not include sugars within intact fruit or those naturally present in milk. WHO recommends less than 10% of total energy from free sugars and conditionally suggests less than 5%.

The glycemic index is not the whole answer

The glycemic index (GI) describes a food's relative effect on blood glucose under standardized conditions. The amount eaten, cooking methods, ripeness, and combinations with protein, fat and fibre also influence an actual meal.

Rather than relying on one number, consider both carbohydrate quality and quantity. Whole grains, legumes and minimally processed foods often offer more fibre. People with diabetes should individualize portions and meal timing around their treatment plan.

Sweet drinks and hidden sugars

Soda, sweetened coffee, bubble tea, fruit-flavoured drinks and some flavoured yogurts can add free sugars almost unnoticed. Drinks can be consumed quickly and may not be as filling as solid foods.

Compare labels and ingredients across similar products. Canada's Food Guide recommends water as the drink of choice and limiting highly processed foods that contribute excess sugars, sodium or saturated fat.

Sustainable habits matter more than prohibition

A sweet treat need not be a moral failure. Sharing cake occasionally is different from replacing water with sweet drinks every day. Repeated daily choices shape eating patterns more than one bite.

Keep vegetables, whole grains, legumes and appropriate protein on the table; choose whole fruit more often than juice; make drinking water routine. If you take insulin or other glucose-lowering medicines, do not drastically change your food intake or medication without clinical guidance because hypoglycemia can occur.

RUNLU · Today's practice

  • Notice what you drink for several days: water, unsweetened tea, sweetened coffee and juice.
  • Replace one sugary drink with water, or choose whole fruit rather than a glass of juice.
  • When buying grains or yogurt, compare fibre, portions and ingredients as well as sugar.

General health education only, not individual medical advice. People with diabetes, gestational glucose disorders or glucose-lowering medication should follow individualized medical or dietitian guidance.
