Water and Life: How Much, and When Should We Drink?

Water is so ordinary that we rarely reflect on it. A glass can relieve thirst, but it cannot cure every illness. Too little may cause problems, while more is not always better. Useful hydration advice begins with the body's needs, changing conditions and individual differences.

Why water matters

Water helps regulate temperature, move nutrients, support metabolism and remove waste. We lose water through breathing, sweating, urine and stool, and must replenish it.

Food also contains water: vegetables, fruit, soup and porridge all contribute. Total water intake includes water from food and all beverages, not just plain drinking water.

Must everyone drink eight glasses a day?

Eight glasses is an easy slogan, not an individual prescription. North American adequate-intake reference values are about 3.7 L of total water daily for adult men and 2.7 L for adult women. These include food and beverages; they are population-based reference values, not mandatory amounts of plain water.

Needs change with body size, activity, weather, diet, age and health. Alberta Health Services gives a practical fluid reference of roughly 9–12 cups daily for most adults, while emphasizing individual differences.

Thirst, urine colour and body signals

For many healthy adults, thirst provides a useful signal. Urine colour may offer clues, but medicines, vitamins and illness can affect it; colour alone cannot diagnose dehydration.

Older adults may notice thirst less reliably. Fever, vomiting, diarrhea and heavy sweating can rapidly increase losses. Severe dizziness, confusion or inability to retain fluids deserves prompt medical attention.

Do tea, coffee and soup count?

Yes. Unsweetened tea, moderate coffee, milk, soup and water-rich foods all contribute fluid. The claim that caffeine cancels out the water in a normal cup of coffee is misleading.

Sweetened coffee, bubble tea and soft drinks can add substantial sugars or energy. Canada's Food Guide recommends water as the drink of choice and includes unsweetened tea and coffee among other healthy drink options.

Heat, exercise and special situations

Working in heat, prolonged exercise and heavy sweating may increase fluid needs. Extended intense exercise can also require attention to sodium and other electrolytes.

Drinking excessive water in a short time can dilute blood sodium and cause hyponatremia. Hydration should account for the weather, duration, sweat losses and individual circumstances rather than a rigid oversized target.

Make hydration an easy routine

Keeping water accessible with meals or when going out can be more sustainable than counting every sip. During and after activity, drink according to your needs and circumstances.

People with heart failure, advanced kidney disease or prescribed fluid restrictions may need very different targets. Follow individualized medical advice, particularly if taking diuretics.

RUNLU · Today's practice

  • Notice where your fluids come from: water, tea, coffee, soup and other drinks.
  • Keep water convenient at meals and during daily activities instead of chasing a universal eight-glass rule.
  • Adjust your approach in hot weather, during exercise or when sick.

General education only; not individualized medical advice. People on fluid restrictions or with serious heart or kidney conditions should follow medical guidance.
