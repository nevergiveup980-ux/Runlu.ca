Why Our Bodies Need to Move: Rediscovering Health Through Everyday Activity

Getting out of bed, opening the curtains and walking to the kitchen for a glass of water may feel unremarkable. Yet each small action involves the quiet coordination of muscles, joints, nerves, heart and lungs. Exercise is often pictured as running or lifting weights, but movement begins in ordinary life. This third season starts with a simple question: why does our body need to move?

The body was never meant to remain completely still

Muscles, joints, heart, lungs and the nervous system work together. Reaching for a cup, opening a door and walking to a shop all rely on this cooperation. Physical activity does not occur only in a gym; it is part of the way we live.

Moving engages muscles and challenges our cardiovascular and nervous systems to respond. Prolonged inactivity may gradually weaken physical capacity, but meaningful benefits do not demand intense training from everyone. Starting with small, manageable activities is worthwhile.

Movement and exercise belong in the same conversation

Walking, taking stairs, doing household chores, cycling for transport and structured strength training all offer opportunities to be active. The World Health Organization emphasizes that physical activity includes movement in daily life, work, transportation and leisure.

Intensity and duration still matter. A relaxed walk places different demands on the body than brisk walking, and housework does not automatically replace strength, balance or endurance training. The most useful approach is to let everyday movement and planned exercise complement each other.

What can a little time each day accomplish?

The WHO recommends that most adults accumulate 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity each week, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination. Muscle-strengthening activities involving major muscle groups are recommended on at least two days each week. These population targets are guides, not a test everyone must pass immediately.

At moderate intensity, breathing generally becomes faster while conversation remains possible. The feeling varies between people. Even adding a short walk or breaking up sitting time can be a sensible beginning for someone who is currently inactive; duration and effort can increase gradually.

Long sitting spells deserve an interruption

Screens, meetings, driving and resting at home may require long periods of sitting. Sitting itself is not a moral failure; the concern is a day with almost no changes in activity. Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines encourage adults to interrupt prolonged sitting and consider movement, sedentary behaviour and sleep together.

Standing for water, walking briefly after work tasks or stretching comfortably are practical ways to bring activity back into the day. People with mobility limitations may also benefit from movements they can do safely in their circumstances.

With age, the goal becomes preserving everyday function

Getting out of a chair, carrying groceries and staying steady on uneven ground can matter more than any sports record. Activity for older adults can include aerobic work, strength exercises and balance practice. The WHO recommends multicomponent activity emphasizing functional balance and strength on several days weekly for older adults.

People differ widely in mobility and health. Some walk briskly; others use a chair, railing or professional support. New chest pain, fainting, marked breathlessness, recent injury or recurrent falls deserve medical evaluation rather than pushing through a difficult workout.

The best beginning is one we can repeat tomorrow

Movement is more than the step count on a watch. A walk to see a friend, a family stroll after dinner or an afternoon in the garden can make activity part of a meaningful life. Sustainable routines respect our time, abilities, interests and surroundings.

In this season we will explore walking, strength, endurance, balance, sedentary time, sleep, body clocks and recovery. The goal is not a punishing training schedule, but a humane rhythm: move when we can, rest when we need to, and preserve our ability to participate in life.

RUNLU · Today's movement practice

  • Notice the longest period you spend sitting today and consider one comfortable, safe movement break.
  • Choose an activity you enjoy and can afford; start at a duration and effort suitable for you.
  • Name a real-life ability you want to preserve—walking outdoors, climbing stairs or carrying groceries—and let that guide your activity.

General health education, not a personal medical or rehabilitation plan. Seek urgent assessment for chest pain, severe breathlessness or fainting; adapt activity with qualified guidance when health conditions, injury or fall risk require it.
