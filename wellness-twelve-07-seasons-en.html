Seasonal Wellness: Tradition and Changing Physiology

How Seasons Affect Us

Daylight, temperature and daily routines change through the year. These shifts can affect sleep timing, mood, activity and the body's ability to regulate heat. There is no universal seasonal routine for every climate or person.

Spring: Start Moving Again

Longer days can make outdoor movement easier. Increase activity gradually after a sedentary winter and consider local pollen and air quality if you have allergies.

Summer: Prevent Heat Illness

In hot weather, prioritize hydration, shade, cool spaces and safer activity times. Sweating is primarily a cooling mechanism, not evidence of special detoxification. Confusion or severe weakness in extreme heat requires urgent attention.

Autumn: Notice Changing Light

Shorter days can affect sleep and time outdoors. Consistent routines, daylight exposure and social activity may help. There is no universal medical requirement for seasonal tonics.

Winter: Mood and Safety

Seasonal affective disorder is a form of depression with a recurring seasonal pattern. Persistent symptoms that affect daily life warrant professional care. Cold weather also brings risks of hypothermia, frostbite and icy falls.

Tradition and Evidence

Traditional advice to adapt to the seasons can encourage awareness, but organ-season correspondences are not modern clinical diagnoses. Practical prevention focuses on sleep, movement, nutrition and safety.

Today's Practice

Notice how this week's daylight and weather relate to your sleep and mood. Choose one safe, sustainable adjustment. Living with the seasons means responding thoughtfully to change.
