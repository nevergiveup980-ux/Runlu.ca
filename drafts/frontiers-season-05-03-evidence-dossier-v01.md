## Editorial question
When a picture, recording or document appears convincing, what must we verify before treating it as evidence? Separate three distinct questions:
1. Provenance: where did this content come from, and how has it been modified?
2. Integrity/authenticity: does the content match its claimed origin or recorded history?
3. Factual truth and context: even authentic media can mislead if staged, cropped, selectively presented or taken out of context.

## Independently located evidence
A. US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), AI 100-4, published 2024-11-20. https://www.nist.gov/publications/reducing-risks-posed-synthetic-content-overview-technical-approaches-digital-content
NIST reviews provenance tracking, watermarking, synthetic content detection, labeling, evaluation and limits. Provenance does not guarantee factual truth. Cite precise pages in article draft after PDF review.

B. Canadian Artificial Intelligence Safety Institute, Research Agenda on Risks from Synthetic Content (2026).
https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/ised/en/canadian-artificial-intelligence-safety-institute/research-agenda-risks-synthetic-content
No single current method reliably protects against unidentified synthetic content; effectiveness varies by modality and context. Watermarking and metadata can fail, be lost, or create privacy concerns. Avoid overstating universal reliability.

C. OECD, Due Diligence Guidance for Responsible AI (2026), Box 2.9.
https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-due-diligence-guidance-for-responsible-ai_41671712-en/full-report/component-4.html
Provenance is hard to determine at Internet scale, metadata can be manipulated, and multi-stakeholder standards such as C2PA are being developed.

D. Mozilla Foundation, In Transparency We Trust? (2024).
https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/research/library/in-transparency-we-trust/research-report/
Visible labels and warnings have human-factors limitations; they are not a comprehensive solution.

## Claims permitted in eventual essay
- Digital provenance can supply useful origin and editing-history signals, but it cannot alone prove the underlying claim true.
- Detectors are probabilistic and may produce false positives and false negatives.
- Human, institutional, and technical verification must complement each other.
- Evidence handling must balance transparency with privacy.

## Claims prohibited without additional proof
- Every genuine image can be authenticated using metadata.
- Absence of a watermark proves content is human-created.
- A provenance signature proves the depicted event occurred as described.
- A single AI detector can decisively establish authenticity.

## Proposed six-section argument (outline only)
1. The image that looked conclusive.
2. Where a file came from is not the same as what happened.
3. Watermarks, metadata, signatures and their limits.
4. How authentic fragments can still tell a false story.
5. What journalists, courts, platforms and ordinary readers need from verification.
6. Trustworthy evidence requires trustworthy practices, not a magic badge.
