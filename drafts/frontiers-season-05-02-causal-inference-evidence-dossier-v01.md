## Candidate title
当我们误把相关当成原因
When Correlation Masquerades as Cause

## Distinctive question
When two things move together, what evidence warrants the conclusion that one causes the other?

## Primary methodological basis
Miguel A. Hernán and James M. Robins, *Causal Inference: What If*, official author site:
https://miguelhernan.org/whatifbook
Publisher overview: https://www.routledge.com/Causal-Inference-What-If/Hernan-Robins/p/book/9781420076165
Relevant topics: defining causal effects, randomized experiments, observational studies, confounding, selection bias, measurement bias, counterfactual outcomes, and explicit assumptions.

## Claims ledger
SUPPORTED: A measured association alone does not establish a causal effect.
SUPPORTED: Randomized allocation can improve causal identification by reducing systematic confounding, though chance, attrition and measurement still matter.
SUPPORTED: Observational causal inference requires assumptions, contextual knowledge, and careful design; it is not impossible in principle.
AVOID: 'Only randomized trials prove causality' (false overstatement).
AVOID: 'All correlations are meaningless' (false).
AVOID: treating a toy example as a verified real-world event.
NEEDS SOURCE: a specific public-policy or healthcare case with original study and limitations.

## Six-part conceptual structure (not article prose)
1. The everyday seduction of two rising lines.
2. Define the causal question and hypothetical intervention.
3. The unseen third variable: confounding.
4. Why selection and measurement can mislead.
5. What randomized trials and well-designed observational studies can—and cannot—tell us.
6. Intellectual humility: conclusions should disclose assumptions.
