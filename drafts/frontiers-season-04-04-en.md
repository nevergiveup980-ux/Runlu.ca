# What Should Education Become?
RUNLU · Frontiers · Season IV · Essay 04 / 10
JOHN ZHANG · English original · Editorial draft

## The Question a Student Cannot Put Into a Search Box

A student sits at a kitchen table with a difficult assignment. A few years ago, she might have searched for an explanation, asked a teacher, or struggled through the first paragraph herself. Now a conversational system can produce a polished answer in seconds.

She reads it, changes a few sentences, and submits it.

Her work is finished. But what, exactly, has she learned?

That question is not an argument against using artificial intelligence in school. A good explanation from a digital tool may help a student who is embarrassed to ask a question in class. A translation may open a subject to someone learning in a second language. An adaptive exercise may reveal a misunderstanding before it becomes a lasting obstacle.

The concern is more precise. If a tool can perform the visible part of learning, how do we make sure the invisible part still happens?

Education has always used tools: books, calculators, laboratories, libraries, and computers. What changes with generative AI is that a tool can now produce the very thing teachers often ask students to create as evidence of understanding.

The future of education therefore cannot be decided by asking only whether schools should permit AI. We must ask what learning is for, what students should be able to do without assistance, and when using assistance is itself an important skill.

## Knowledge Still Matters

It is fashionable to say that memorizing facts is pointless because information is always available. There is a grain of truth in the argument. Students should not spend their entire education reproducing details that can be looked up in moments.

But knowing something and knowing where to find it are not the same.

A reader who understands history can recognize when a convenient explanation leaves out the people who suffered its consequences. Someone with a working grasp of science is better equipped to notice when a confident answer contradicts established evidence. A person who can estimate quantities mentally has a better chance of catching a calculation that looks precise but makes no sense.

We need knowledge inside our minds to ask useful questions of the knowledge outside them.

That does not mean every student must memorize the same endless catalogue of dates, formulas, and definitions. It means education must distinguish between foundational understanding and details that can reasonably be retrieved. The balance will differ across ages and disciplines.

A future doctor needs more than the ability to ask a system for a diagnosis. A future electrician needs more than an app that describes a circuit. In both cases, knowledge supports judgment when the tool is unavailable, mistaken, or confronted with circumstances it does not understand.

AI can make information easier to reach. It cannot relieve us of the responsibility to understand enough to use that information wisely.

## The Skills That Do Not Fit Neatly on a Test

Consider two students who receive the same assignment: investigate why a neighborhood park floods after heavy rain.

One produces an elegant report using online sources. The other walks through the park, speaks to residents, checks where the water collects, discovers that one explanation does not match the evidence, and revises an earlier assumption.

Both may produce good writing. Yet the second student has practiced something that a finished report alone may not reveal: observation, listening, uncertainty, and the willingness to change course.

These capacities are difficult to reduce to a multiple-choice score. They are also central to life beyond school.

Students need to learn how to disagree without humiliating one another, how to cooperate with someone whose strengths differ from their own, and how to continue when the first attempt fails. They need to recognize the limits of their knowledge and the consequences of their decisions.

Creativity matters too, but not as a slogan. It can mean finding a safer way to organize a workshop, explaining a difficult idea to a younger child, or noticing that a problem has been framed incorrectly.

Schools cannot teach all of this through lectures alone. Students need projects, discussion, experimentation, feedback, and opportunities to make manageable mistakes.

An education that rewards only the smooth final answer may become less useful precisely when machines become better at producing smooth final answers.

## Teaching With AI, Not Surrendering to It

There is no single rule that fits every classroom. A beginning reader may need to work through a passage without an automated summary. A student learning a foreign language may benefit from conversational practice with a system that never grows impatient. An advanced student may use AI to challenge a draft, explore competing arguments, or test a computer program.

The important distinction is between support that develops ability and substitution that conceals its absence.

A teacher might ask students to show their reasoning, explain why they rejected a suggestion, compare an AI-generated response with a primary source, or defend a conclusion in conversation. Some assignments may be completed with tools; others may deliberately require independent work. Both can be educational when their purpose is clear.

Teachers also need time, training, and the authority to make professional judgments. Simply handing a new platform to an already overworked staff does not constitute a strategy.

Nor should a school's excitement about personalization erase the importance of privacy. Systems that collect student writing, behavior, or sensitive details require careful choices about consent, data retention, security, and who can access the information. Children should not have to surrender unnecessary personal data to receive a good education.

We should also be cautious about automated judgments of students. A model may offer useful feedback, but consequential decisions about ability, discipline, or opportunity deserve human oversight and a way to challenge errors.

The goal is not to place a machine between every student and every question. It is to use tools where they help, while protecting the relationships and responsibilities that make teaching human.

## Opportunity Cannot Depend on a Subscription

The promise of AI-assisted learning sounds different in a well-funded school and in a community where reliable internet access is still a struggle.

Some students have quiet rooms, capable devices, paid services, and adults who can help them judge what a system says. Others share a phone, have limited connectivity, or must work after school. If advanced learning tools become essential but remain unevenly accessible, technology could widen gaps that education is supposed to narrow.

Access is not only a question of hardware. Language support, disability accommodations, local relevance, teacher preparation, and the quality of materials all matter. A system trained mainly on the experiences of distant populations may overlook the history, culture, or practical realities of the children using it.

Public institutions should be able to examine educational tools rather than accept marketing claims as proof of effectiveness. Schools need procurement standards, evidence of learning outcomes, accessible alternatives, and safeguards against locking families into recurring costs.

A useful tool is not automatically a fair one.

We should measure educational progress by whether more students can think, participate, and pursue meaningful opportunities—not merely by the number of accounts activated or minutes spent on a platform.

## Preparing People for More Than Their First Job

Education must respond to changes in work, but it must not become nothing more than training for the next hiring trend.

A child entering school today may work in occupations that change repeatedly over a lifetime. No curriculum can predict every tool that person will encounter. Schools can, however, cultivate habits that make continued learning possible: reading carefully, reasoning with evidence, asking questions, working with others, and accepting correction.

Adults need those opportunities too. A society that tells displaced workers to retrain while making further education expensive or impossible to fit around family responsibilities has not solved the problem. Learning throughout life requires flexible routes, credible qualifications, and support that respects the realities of working people.

There is also a civic purpose to education. Citizens must evaluate public claims, understand competing interests, recognize manipulation, and take part in decisions that affect their communities. A person can be highly employable and still be poorly prepared for these responsibilities.

And education has a personal purpose that cannot be measured in wages. Literature can deepen our understanding of another life. Music and art can give form to feelings we struggle to name. History can remind us that our present arrangements were made by people and can be changed by people.

The first essay of this season argued that the future is not predetermined. The second asked who shapes AI. The third asked how the benefits of new productivity might be shared. Education connects all three questions: it influences who can participate in shaping the future, and who is left merely adapting to decisions made elsewhere.

Perhaps the most important question a school can teach a student is not “What is the answer?”

It is “How do you know—and what will you do with what you have learned?”
