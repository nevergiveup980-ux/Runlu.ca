## One-sentence thesis
A wastewater sample can reveal community-level pathogen signals, but it becomes useful public-health intelligence only through careful sampling, laboratory analysis, timely reporting, comparison with other evidence, and accountable decisions.

## English-first article architecture
1. **Opening scene: The sample no one sees.** An explicitly illustrative, non-documentary municipal collection scene; avoid invented quotes or specific observed incidents.
2. **What enters the sewer.** Biological shedding, community catchments, limits of who is represented. Avoid suggesting identifiable household-level inference.
3. **From water to measurement.** Collection, transport, molecular testing, quality control and signal normalization. Distinguish RNA signal from case count.
4. **The clocks that matter.** Infection, shedding, collection, lab processing, dissemination and decision. Introduce Québec program's 13.1-day vs 8-day estimated medians, with explicit setting/method caveats and missing symptom onset information.
5. **When the signal helps.** PHAC monitoring, cross-checks with clinical evidence; Alberta study as evidence of trend tracking, not universal lead-time prediction.
6. **When the signal misleads.** Rain/snowmelt, industrial flow, variable shedding, sampling bias, variant/pathogen and model uncertainties.
7. **Whose data and whose benefit?** WHO 2025 published ethics framework: aims, benefits, harms, proportionality, equity, respect for persons, data/sample stewardship. Do not say it is still under development.
8. **What a responsible alert looks like.** Public communication distinguishes observed signal, interpretation, uncertainty and recommended action. Do not fabricate a government decision or recommend medical actions beyond official guidance.
9. **Closing: A signal is not a verdict.** Revisit the opening; scientific restraint can increase trust.
