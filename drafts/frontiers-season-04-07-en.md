# When Machines Help Make Decisions
RUNLU · Frontiers · Season IV · Essay 07 / 10
JOHN ZHANG · English original · Editorial draft

## The Decision Behind the Screen

A man applies for a job he believes he can do well. He has the experience, the patience, and the practical knowledge the work requires. A few days later, an automated message tells him that his application will not move forward.

He wonders whether anyone read it.

Perhaps a person reviewed his qualifications carefully. Perhaps software helped organize a crowded list of candidates. Or perhaps a model ranked him below a threshold that no one has explained.

From the outside, these possibilities can look exactly the same: a polite rejection on a screen.

Machines have assisted human decisions for a long time. Calculators help us compare costs. Medical instruments reveal details the eye cannot see. Statistical tools help governments plan services. The arrival of more capable artificial intelligence does not make every use of automated assistance fundamentally new.

What changes is the range of judgments that software can now influence—and the ease with which its recommendations can be mistaken for conclusions.

A system can summarize a record, identify a pattern, estimate a risk, or suggest an option. These abilities may be valuable. But a recommendation is not the same as a reasoned judgment, and a numerical score is not the same as a person's worth.

When a decision affects someone's livelihood, health, freedom, or future, the question is not simply whether a machine was involved.

It is whether the process deserves the trust placed in it.

## What Machines Can See—and What They Miss

Imagine a busy hospital where clinicians must decide which patients need urgent attention. A carefully evaluated system might help detect warning signs in complex information. It might draw attention to a pattern a tired professional would otherwise overlook.

That is a meaningful possibility, not a reason to hand over the entire decision.

A model learns from available data, which may be incomplete, inconsistent, or shaped by past human choices. It may perform well in the setting where it was developed and less well among different patients, institutions, or circumstances. A confident-looking result can conceal uncertainty.

Human judgment is imperfect too. Professionals become tired, make assumptions, overlook evidence, and sometimes allow prejudice to affect their decisions. We should not romanticize the unaided human decision-maker.

The important comparison is not between flawless people and flawed machines, or flawless machines and flawed people. It is between actual processes, with their strengths, weaknesses, and consequences.

In medicine, the relevant questions include whether a tool improves outcomes in the intended setting, whether it is safe for the people who will use it, and whether clinicians can recognize when its advice is unreliable. In recruitment, we must ask whether a ranking measures relevant skills or merely reproduces patterns from previous hiring. In lending, we must examine whether the factors used are appropriate and whether errors can be corrected.

A system may be accurate on average and still fail particular groups or unusual cases. An individual who falls outside a familiar pattern is not a statistical inconvenience. That person is precisely where responsible judgment may matter most.

Good assistance expands our ability to notice. Bad assistance gives us an excuse to stop looking.

## The Problem of Borrowed Authority

There is a quiet temptation in automated decision-making: when a recommendation arrives wrapped in technical language, people may give it more authority than it has earned.

A manager sees a score. A teacher sees a risk flag. A public employee sees a suggested classification. If the system is described as intelligent or objective, challenging it can feel like challenging mathematics itself.

Yet every system reflects choices. Someone decided what problem to solve, which data to collect, how to define success, what errors to tolerate, and when a prediction should influence action.

These are not purely technical questions. They are also ethical, institutional, and sometimes political questions.

Consider a tool designed to identify people who might need additional support. If its result is used to offer timely help, it may be beneficial. If the same result is treated as a permanent label or a reason to exclude someone, the consequences can be very different.

The danger grows when human review exists only on paper. A person may technically be allowed to override the system but lack the time, information, training, or institutional permission to do so. The decision is then described as human-led even though the machine's recommendation is almost always followed.

Meaningful oversight is not a decorative signature at the bottom of a form. It requires the ability to question, investigate, and sometimes refuse the recommendation.

And that ability must be supported by the organization, not left to the courage of one employee.

## When Something Goes Wrong, Who Answers?

Suppose an automated system contributes to a harmful decision. A qualified applicant is rejected for the wrong reason. A patient receives inappropriate priority. Someone loses access to an essential service because of an incorrect record.

Who is responsible?

It is tempting to point to the software. But software cannot explain itself in a hearing, compensate an injured person, or accept the obligations of a public office. Responsibility must remain with the people and institutions that choose, deploy, supervise, and rely on the system.

That does not mean every person involved bears the same responsibility. Developers may be accountable for defects they could reasonably have prevented. Organizations may be responsible for testing, procurement, training, monitoring, and proper use. Regulators and public authorities may have duties to establish and enforce safeguards. The precise obligations depend on the setting and applicable law.

What matters is that responsibility does not vanish into the space between these actors.

People affected by consequential decisions need practical avenues for explanation and correction. They should be able to know when automated systems play a significant role where disclosure is appropriate, challenge factual errors, and seek a review with real authority to change the outcome.

An appeal that cannot alter anything is not much of an appeal.

Institutions also need records sufficient to investigate failures, without collecting unlimited personal information in the name of accountability. That tension is real. As we considered in the previous essay, privacy is not an obstacle to be brushed aside whenever data might be useful.

Trust is built not by promising that systems will never fail, but by showing what happens when they do.

## Designing a Partnership Worth Trusting

A sensible approach begins by asking how much authority a system should have in a particular task.

For some low-stakes activities, automation can save time with little risk. Sorting routine documents or suggesting a meeting time may require only straightforward checks. For decisions that affect someone's health, employment, financial security, legal rights, or access to public services, stronger evidence and safeguards are necessary.

The appropriate role of a machine can vary: it may organize information, flag cases for attention, offer a second opinion, or carry out a narrowly defined action under clear controls. In some situations, automated decisions may be legally restricted or inappropriate altogether.

Before deploying a consequential system, institutions should define its purpose, test it with relevant data, examine errors across affected populations, and decide what level of human review is needed. They should monitor performance after deployment, because real life changes and a model that once worked may become less reliable.

They should also ask a simpler question: is this tool solving a genuine problem, or merely making an existing process faster without making it fairer?

A faster mistake is still a mistake.

Human reviewers need enough information to understand the recommendation, enough time to examine unusual cases, and enough authority to disagree. People subject to the decision need understandable explanations and effective ways to raise concerns. Independent evaluation can help identify problems that internal enthusiasm overlooks.

None of this guarantees perfect decisions. It creates a process capable of learning from mistakes without treating the people harmed as acceptable collateral.

The goal is not to keep a human in the loop as a slogan. It is to keep human responsibility alive in practice.

## The Future We Must Still Choose

There is a difference between asking a machine for help and asking it to decide what matters.

A system can estimate how likely an outcome is. It cannot, by prediction alone, determine what risks a society should accept, whose needs deserve priority, or how competing rights should be balanced. Those questions require public values, legitimate authority, and a willingness to answer for consequences.

We will sometimes need machines to help us make better decisions. Complex problems can overwhelm unaided attention. Evidence can be scattered across more information than any one person can read. Refusing useful assistance simply because it comes from software would not serve people well.

But neither should we confuse the ability to calculate with the authority to govern.

A fair society needs decisions that can be questioned. It needs room for context, exceptions, mercy, and correction. It needs institutions willing to explain not only what a system recommended but why they believed following it was justified.

The man who received that rejection email may never know whether a machine played a role. Perhaps the employer made a careful choice. Perhaps the process failed him. What he deserves is not a promise that every application will succeed, but confidence that the process considered relevant evidence, treated him fairly, and allowed errors to be addressed.

The central question is therefore not whether machines will participate in important decisions. They already do, in many forms.

The question is whether we will design those decisions so that technology strengthens human judgment—or quietly replaces responsibility with an answer no one is prepared to defend.
