# Chapter Eight — How Science Tests a Wellness Claim

*The Universe Within Us — English adaptation, working draft*

## The Promise on the Bottle

Imagine a fictional drink called Morning Glow. Its advertisement promises more energy, stronger immunity, and a younger body within four weeks. A smiling customer appears beside the headline “80% satisfied.”

The drink and every statistic in this example are invented for teaching. They describe no real product.

The advertisement offers a familiar story: a problem, an effortless solution, and a happy ending. Science begins by breaking that story into questions. What does “energy” mean? How was fatigue measured? What would count as improved immune function? Does satisfaction demonstrate a medical benefit?

A claim that cannot be defined cannot be tested reliably.

## Define the Outcome Before Seeing the Results

Feeling better might mean sleeping longer, having less daytime fatigue, functioning better at work, or simply enjoying a product. These are not interchangeable outcomes.

Researchers should specify in advance what they intend to measure and how. Subjective experiences such as pain and fatigue are legitimate research outcomes, but they still require appropriate instruments and careful comparisons. A single immune marker, meanwhile, cannot establish a sweeping claim that someone's immunity has been “boosted.”

When marketers can redefine success after hearing a customer's story, every result becomes an advertisement and none becomes a meaningful test.

## Eighty Percent of Whom?

Suppose one hundred people try a product, but only twenty answer a follow-up survey. Sixteen say they are satisfied. The advertiser can truthfully calculate that 80 percent of respondents were satisfied. It would be misleading to imply that eighty of the original hundred improved.

The denominator matters. So do nonresponses and the reasons for satisfaction. Some customers may like the flavor or convenience without noticing any health change.

Most importantly, what would have happened without the product? A satisfaction survey cannot answer that causal question.

### Relative and Absolute Change

Consider another invented example. An event occurs in 2 percent of a comparison group and 1 percent of a treatment group. That is a 50 percent relative reduction, but a one-percentage-point absolute reduction. Both descriptions are mathematically valid. They convey different practical impressions.

Whenever an advertisement announces a dramatic percentage, ask for the starting rate, actual numbers, comparison group, and uncertainty.

## Observation Is Not Proof of Cause

Suppose regular buyers of Morning Glow report greater energy than nonbuyers. Perhaps the drink helped. Perhaps buyers already had more flexible schedules, higher incomes, or greater interest in wellness. Perhaps energetic people were more inclined to buy it.

These are examples of confounding and possible reverse causation. Statistical adjustment may help, but unmeasured differences can remain.

Association can generate a hypothesis. Establishing causation requires stronger design and careful reasoning.

## What a Randomized Trial Adds

When ethical and feasible, researchers may randomly assign eligible participants to an intervention and a suitable comparison. Randomization aims to reduce systematic differences between groups [14]. Blinding, where possible, can reduce expectation-related influences on reporting and assessment.

A credible trial also specifies eligibility, dose, duration, primary outcomes, adverse events, and how results will be analyzed. Researchers must account for participants who do not follow the assigned intervention and for missing outcome data. Simply announcing an “intention-to-treat analysis” does not resolve every problem.

Trials can still mislead if they are too small, too short, poorly conducted, or selectively reported. Checking trial registration and prespecified analysis plans helps reveal whether researchers highlighted only favorable outcomes or time points. Some interventions cannot be fully blinded; that limitation should be acknowledged and addressed rather than concealed.

## Does a Statistically Significant Result Matter?

In a fictional trial, 10 percent of the comparison group and 12 percent of the drink group meet a predefined improvement threshold. The absolute difference is two percentage points—about two additional people per hundred.

An advertisement might describe this as a 20 percent relative increase. Yet a reader still needs to know the sample size, uncertainty interval, risk of bias, and whether the improvement is meaningful in everyday life.

Statistical significance does not automatically imply clinical importance. Failure to reach statistical significance does not automatically establish that a treatment has no effect.

## One Study Is Not the End

An isolated positive study should prompt further questions. Do independent researchers find similar results? Were unfavorable studies left unpublished? Did the funder influence design, analysis, or publication?

Industry funding does not make a result automatically false, but transparency about conflicts and research decisions matters.

Systematic reviews assess the wider body of evidence. Even a well-conducted review may conclude that certainty is low because the underlying studies are weak or inconsistent. “Insufficient evidence of benefit” is not identical to “proof of no benefit.”

Laboratory findings, animal experiments, and human clinical outcomes also answer different questions. A change in cells in a dish is not proof that drinking a product prolongs human life.

## Risks and Opportunity Costs

Even a modest benefit must be weighed against adverse effects, interactions, price, and alternatives. Spending heavily on a supplement may crowd out nutritious food or necessary care. Treating persistent fatigue as a simple lack of vitality may delay evaluation of a medical problem.

The question is not merely “Could this work?” It is “For whom, compared with what, at what cost and risk?”

## Six Questions to Carry Away

Before accepting a wellness claim, ask what outcome is promised; who was studied; whether a credible comparison was used; how large the absolute effect was; which harms and limitations were reported; and whether independent evidence supports the finding.

Science changes its conclusions when better evidence arrives. That is a strength, not an excuse to treat every claim as equally credible.

Those questions become especially important when a study's published headline tells a simpler story than its results.

## A Study That Disappeared from the Headline

Consider an invented research project with three planned outcomes: fatigue, sleep quality, and work performance. At the end, the results are mixed. Fatigue shows little difference between groups, sleep results are uncertain, and one work-performance measure appears favorable.

A press release highlights only the favorable measure and announces that the intervention “improves daily vitality.” A reader may never learn that the other outcomes were examined.

This illustrates selective reporting. A trial can be randomized and still communicate a distorted story if outcomes are chosen for publicity after results are known. Looking at a preregistered protocol, a published statistical plan, and the complete results helps distinguish the original question from a later marketing narrative.

There is a further difference between choosing the most flattering number from several analyses and failing to publish an entire planned outcome. Both deserve scrutiny, but they may require different sources to investigate. A journal abstract alone is not always the whole record.

## When Two Good-Looking Studies Disagree

One trial reports improvement. Another reports little difference. This does not automatically mean that science is unreliable or that someone has concealed the truth.

Perhaps the studies enrolled different populations, used different doses, followed participants for different lengths of time, or defined improvement differently. Random variation and methodological weaknesses can also matter.

The first task is to check whether the studies actually asked the same question. The next is to consider their risk of bias and the precision of their estimates. A synthesis of evidence can be informative, but combining unlike studies without care may obscure rather than clarify the answer.

An honest conclusion may be that the benefit is plausible but uncertain, or that the evidence is too inconsistent for a confident recommendation. Uncertainty is an outcome of careful analysis, not necessarily a failure to analyze.

## The Difference Between a Marker and a Life

Imagine a product that changes a laboratory marker after eight weeks. The result may be interesting. But people generally care about whether they can function better, experience fewer symptoms, avoid serious illness, or live longer and healthier lives.

A marker can sometimes serve as a useful surrogate for a clinical outcome, but that relationship must be validated in the relevant context. A number moving in a desirable direction does not automatically show that a person is healthier.

This is especially important in discussions of immunity and aging, where many biological measures are scientifically intriguing but do not by themselves justify broad consumer promises.

Good evidence connects what researchers can measure with what matters to patients—and acknowledges when that connection has not yet been established.

## Who Can Use the Result?

Suppose a trial enrolls healthy adults in their twenties and thirties. Even if the results are convincing for that group, they may not apply directly to older adults with several chronic conditions, pregnant people, or children.

External validity asks whether a finding travels beyond the study setting. Dose, access, adherence, coexisting treatment, and real-world cost may alter what happens outside a controlled experiment.

A responsible recommendation states not just whether something worked in a study, but for whom it was studied and which important questions remain unanswered.

The scientific habit is not to demand perfection before acting. It is to match the strength of a claim to the strength and relevance of its supporting evidence.
