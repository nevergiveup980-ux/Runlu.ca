### The Weekend Clock

A student rises early for weekday classes, then sleeps much later on weekends. On Sunday evening, sleep seems reluctant to arrive; Monday morning feels like crossing a time zone without leaving town.

Researchers sometimes use the term *social jet lag* for a mismatch between social schedules and biological timing. The phrase can help describe a pattern, but it is not a diagnosis for everyone who sleeps late. Sleep duration, individual differences, work demands, and health circumstances complicate the picture.

It would be tempting to answer with a command to keep exactly the same hours every day. Yet students, caregivers, and workers do not always control their schedules. A useful discussion of biological rhythms should illuminate those constraints rather than turn them into moral shortcomings.

Editorial decision: keep the existing light and night-shift sections. In Chapter Nine, retain practical schedule design rather than repeating the mechanism of circadian timing.

## Chapter Four: existing coverage

The current manuscript already explains the thermostat analogy, the limitations of a single measurement, normal variability, consumer devices, and vague commercial balance claims. The separate expansion notes largely duplicate this material.

Recommended new paragraph for the "Adaptation Has Limits and Costs" section:

The language of adaptation can also conceal a social question: who is expected to keep adapting, and at whose expense? A workplace may praise resilience while offering little control over schedules or recovery. The body's capacity to respond to demands is remarkable, but it does not establish that those demands are fair or sustainable. Physiological explanation and social responsibility belong in the same conversation, without being mistaken for the same kind of evidence.

Editorial decision: do not repeat the clinical-trial checklist from Chapter Eight. Keep Chapter Four focused on physiological regulation and the limits of analogy.
