# Chapter Seven — Understanding Traditional Medicine Without Mythologizing It

*The Universe Within Us — English adaptation, working draft*

## An Old Medicine Cabinet and a New Advertisement

In a museum, an old wooden medicine cabinet holds drawers marked with handwritten names. The worn handles evoke generations of people trying to understand illness and ease suffering. Outside, an advertisement invokes a “thousand-year formula” and promises dramatic results for a product sold today.

The two scenes should not be confused. A tradition can be historically important without proving that a modern product works. A particular medical explanation can lack modern evidence without erasing the cultural meaning of the documents and practices that preserve it.

Respect requires curiosity rather than unquestioning belief or easy ridicule.

## Tradition Has a History

Traditional medicine was never a single, unchanged object. Methods, materials, vocabulary, and interpretations differed across periods, regions, and practitioners. Later generations organized and revised what they inherited.

When someone says a remedy has “always” been used, useful questions include: In which century? According to what source? In what preparation and dose? For which purpose? Is the present product actually comparable?

An ancient name is not a clinical study. The historical complexity of a tradition deserves more than a marketing slogan.

## Personal Experience and Causation

A neighbor reports sleeping better after trying a traditional practice. The improvement may be genuine and deeply meaningful. Listening respectfully is appropriate.

But two questions remain distinct: Did sleep improve, and did this particular practice cause the improvement?

Symptoms fluctuate. Stress, medication, environment, expectations, and other care may change simultaneously. A person may start a new treatment when symptoms are at their worst and later improve partly because the condition returns toward its usual level.

None of these possibilities proves that a treatment does not work. They show why a single story cannot establish causation. Individual experiences can help researchers identify important questions and outcomes, while controlled comparisons are needed to evaluate effectiveness.

We can respect the storyteller without treating the story as a universal prescription.

## Translating *Qi* Carefully

Some modern explanations equate the traditional concept of *qi* directly with electricity, oxygen, or energy in physics. Others describe meridians as though their exact anatomical identity had been conclusively established.

These are different conceptual languages. Historical uses of *qi* are varied and context-dependent. Modern physical quantities have specific definitions and measurements. Similar metaphors of flow do not establish scientific equivalence.

A proposed connection must generate clear, testable predictions and survive independent investigation. Historical concepts do not need to masquerade as modern measurements to have intellectual value.

## Test the Specific Claim

Labels such as acupuncture, herbal medicine, and manual therapy encompass different procedures, materials, doses, practitioners, and intended uses. Evidence for one version or condition cannot automatically validate all the others.

An evaluation should ask: What exactly is the intervention? Who receives it? What is the comparison? Which outcomes are measured? How long does follow-up last? What harms are reported? How reliable are the results?

A finding about one symptom cannot justify claims to treat unrelated diseases. Equally, a disappointing result in one study should not be generalized carelessly to every historical practice.

Scientific fairness depends on specificity.

## Natural Does Not Mean Risk-Free

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health cautions against assuming that natural products are automatically safe or effective [9]. Natural substances may have biological effects, interact with medications, or vary in quality and concentration. Contamination and adulteration are additional concerns.

Regulatory categories and requirements vary by jurisdiction. A product's legal sale does not establish every advertised treatment claim. Traditional use, manufacturing quality, and clinical effectiveness are separate questions.

People who are pregnant, managing chronic illness, preparing for surgery, or taking medication may need particular care. Discussing all supplements and traditional products with a qualified healthcare professional can help identify potential risks and interactions [16].

Replacing timely, effective medical care with an unproven intervention can also cause harm through delay.

### Reading a Label as a Set of Questions

A bottle described as “herbal” may provide ingredients, doses, a manufacturer, and warnings. These details are useful, but a label cannot necessarily answer every question about quality, effectiveness, or suitability.

Can the ingredients and amounts be identified? Is the product traceable? Are claims limited to what evidence supports? What does the relevant regulator actually authorize? Does the person's medical situation change the risk?

Transparent limits and safety information strengthen trust. They are not signs of weakness.

## Whole-Person Care Is Not Owned by One Tradition

It is sometimes claimed that traditional medicine sees the whole person while modern medicine studies only isolated parts. That contrast overlooks modern research on psychology, social conditions, environmental health, and interactions among body systems. It also overlooks the many specific symptoms and interventions described in historical traditions.

The appropriate scale depends on the question. An acute medical emergency may require rapid, precise assessment. Long-term difficulties with sleep, work, or daily functioning may call for a broader understanding of circumstances alongside appropriate clinical care.

Breadth and precision are partners, not enemies.

## Culture Need Not Prove a Cure

A medical manuscript can be a treasured historical document. A family practice can preserve memory. Traditional language about moderation or seasonal awareness can invite reflection.

Such cultural meaning does not depend on proving a miraculous therapeutic effect. Nor does cultural respect exempt medical claims from scrutiny.

History asks how ideas developed and what they meant. Clinical research asks whether a specific intervention produces benefits that outweigh harms. Confusing these questions serves neither patients nor tradition.

## Ancient Words, Modern Packaging

An advertisement may claim “ancestral” origins without explaining which text or regional practice it refers to. Even a genuine historical reference cannot establish that a present-day formula has identical ingredients, processing, concentration, or uses.

We should distinguish an account of history from a claim about a product sold today. The latter needs evidence relevant to today's product and today's patients.

A tradition worthy of study does not need to become a shield for unsupported marketing.

## A Conversation That Can Continue

Respect for tradition and commitment to evidence can coexist. Some specific practices may find support in carefully conducted studies; other claims may remain unsupported or uncertain. Conclusions should reflect the strength of the evidence, not an obligation to praise or condemn an entire category.

Listening to experience is valuable. Testing causal claims is equally valuable. Keeping cultural significance, clinical effectiveness, and safety distinct makes the discussion more honest.

That distinction matters most when a treatment seems to help. The next question is how to tell whether the treatment itself made the difference.

## What Counts as a Fair Comparison?

Imagine that a clinic offers a traditional treatment to people with recurring discomfort. Most visitors have already tried other approaches, and many arrive during an especially difficult period. A month later, some report improvement.

This is worth noticing, but it is not yet a fair estimate of the treatment's effect. People who seek the clinic may differ from those who do not. Symptoms may fluctuate. Other treatments, expectations, and the attention received during visits may all influence outcomes.

A credible comparison should ask what would have happened to similar people without the particular intervention, or with another appropriate intervention. The comparison must be ethical and relevant. For some conditions, usual care may be an important part of both groups; for others, a different comparator is appropriate.

No single research design resolves every question. A randomized trial can help with efficacy under specified conditions, while observational evidence may help investigate uncommon harms or patterns of real-world use. Each type of evidence must be interpreted according to what it can reasonably establish.

## A Remedy Is More Than Its Name

Consider two products bearing the same traditional label. One is a whole-herb preparation; the other is a concentrated extract. Their doses, composition, manufacturing processes, and potential interactions may differ substantially.

Even where historical sources describe a similarly named remedy, it would be careless to assume equivalence without examining the details. Plant identity, sourcing, contamination controls, preparation methods, and ingredient concentrations all matter.

This is not an argument against studying traditional formulations. It is an argument for studying the formulation that people will actually use.

The same precision applies to procedures. A broad category such as acupuncture or manual therapy may include different techniques, treatment schedules, and practitioner training. Evidence should follow the actual intervention rather than the prestige of a category name.

## The Conversation in the Examination Room

A patient has been using a traditional preparation but hesitates to tell their clinician. They worry that the practice will be dismissed as superstition. The clinician, unaware of the product, considers prescribing a new medication.

Silence is not a good outcome for either person. The patient's beliefs and experience deserve a respectful hearing, and the clinician needs an accurate account of substances and treatments that could affect safety.

A better conversation separates cultural meaning from medical claims. What does the patient value about the practice? What benefit have they noticed? What ingredients are involved? Are there known interactions, adverse effects, or reasons to avoid it? Does the patient have symptoms requiring assessment?

Respectful communication does not require clinicians to endorse unsupported claims. It requires them to listen carefully and explain their reasoning without contempt.

## An Honest Answer Can Be Incomplete

People often want a simple verdict: Does traditional medicine work, yes or no? But that question combines too many different interventions, conditions, outcomes, and risks to produce a meaningful single answer.

The more responsible answer may be that evidence supports a narrowly defined use, that another claim lacks convincing evidence, and that a third question remains uncertain.

That unevenness is not inconsistency. It is what happens when conclusions follow evidence instead of loyalty to a label.

Tradition can remain culturally alive while its clinical claims are examined one by one. Indeed, refusing exaggerated promises may be one of the best ways to preserve room for serious research and genuine understanding.
