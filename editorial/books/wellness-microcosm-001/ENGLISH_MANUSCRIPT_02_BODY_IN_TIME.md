# Chapter Two — The Body in Time: Sleep, Seasons, and Circadian Rhythms

*The Universe Within Us — English adaptation, draft 1*

## Two Kinds of Time

At six in the morning, light is spreading across an airport window. A traveler who has crossed several time zones waits for a suitcase. The clock says the day has begun, but their body seems to remain in the middle of the night.

Nothing about this experience necessarily indicates illness or a lack of discipline. Social time and biological time have temporarily fallen out of step. Social time is written into flight schedules, work shifts, school timetables, and calendars. Biological time is expressed through patterns of sleep, alertness, body temperature, and many other physiological activities.

The two systems usually cooperate, but they are not identical. Modern life allows people to work at night, sleep during the day, and travel across continents in hours. That freedom makes coordination more difficult. Instead of merely instructing people to “follow nature,” we should ask how bodies detect time, which conditions alter their rhythms, and what choices people can realistically make.

## The Body Does Not Contain a Simple Clock

The suprachiasmatic nucleus, a small region in the hypothalamus, helps coordinate human circadian timing. Light detected through the eyes provides important information about the external day. Many tissues also have molecular timing processes of their own. The National Institute of General Medical Sciences explains these basic relationships in the source cited in the Chinese edition [10].

Calling this network a body clock is convenient, but it risks suggesting a mechanical device with one set of hands. Biological timing involves several interacting processes. The pressure to sleep generally increases with time awake, while circadian rhythms influence when alertness and sleepiness are more likely. These influences do not always point in the same direction.

Imagine a city whose transportation, water supply, and emergency services have different daily patterns. The city operates through coordination, not by allowing one department to work while every other department closes. The comparison helps us think about the body, but it is not a literal diagram of physiology.

To identify a genuinely internal rhythm, researchers must distinguish it from changes caused by behavior and environment. Observing that someone sleeps at night is not, by itself, enough to establish the mechanism. Studying whether a new schedule improves health requires yet another kind of evidence: a meaningful comparison and outcomes that matter to people.

## Light as a Time Signal

Two neighbors may encounter the same dawn in opposite ways. One opens the curtains before beginning the day. The other, returning from a night shift, closes blackout blinds to protect a period of sleep.

Light helps synchronize circadian timing, but the practical value of light exposure depends on context. Bright light late at night can interfere with some routines. Appropriately timed light may support others. Shift patterns, travel, latitude, and health circumstances all matter.

At northern latitudes, winter days can be short and summer evenings unusually long. It makes little sense to prescribe one universal waking hour as if daylight were identical everywhere. Nor should the importance of natural light be mistaken for permission to ignore ultraviolet exposure, extreme heat, smoke, or eye safety.

## When the Plane Arrives Before the Body

Our traveler has reached a destination, but internal timing takes longer to adjust. Jet lag can affect sleep and daytime functioning because the local light-dark cycle no longer matches the traveler's established rhythms.

Adjustment differs with the number and direction of time zones crossed, recent sleep, light exposure, and individual circumstances. There is no single rule that guarantees immediate adaptation. Persistent or severe symptoms deserve attention rather than automatic dismissal as ordinary jet lag.

This experience makes something usually invisible easier to see: geography can change abruptly, while physiology adjusts through a process.

## The People Who Work While Others Sleep

Hospitals, transportation systems, cleaning services, warehouses, and emergency response depend on work outside conventional daylight hours. Advice that everyone must be asleep by a fixed evening hour ignores these workers and the public services they sustain.

Night work can make rest more difficult. Daytime noise, household responsibilities, commuting, and changing shifts may reduce opportunities for recovery. Yet the response cannot consist only of telling workers to develop stronger willpower.

Consider a household where one adult works nights, another works days, and a child needs care. The family's schedule is a negotiation among competing responsibilities. Useful questions concern the safety of the journey home, the possibility of uninterrupted sleep, the bedroom environment, and whether an employer can reduce fatigue through better scheduling.

Circadian science can identify risks. Acting on that knowledge may require workplace policies, family cooperation, and professional support. A problem partly created by institutions should not be presented solely as a personal failure.

## Sleep Is More Than a Number

Sleep has duration, continuity, regularity, and a subjective dimension: how rested and functional a person feels. Watches and apps can estimate aspects of sleep, but their scores are not diagnoses.

Guidance about sleep needs is useful, yet no single number describes every age group, medical circumstance, or individual. Persistent insomnia, significant daytime sleepiness, or signs of disturbed breathing during sleep may call for clinical assessment. Habits matter, but they cannot substitute for evaluating a sleep disorder.

One difficult night is not a moral defeat. Health education should reduce confusion rather than make people anxious about every imperfect score.

## Seasons Change Circumstances, Not Rules for Everyone

Winter and summer change more than temperature. They alter daylight, travel conditions, opportunities for outdoor activity, and exposure to hazards. Someone facing extreme heat may need a different exercise schedule; someone facing ice and darkness may need a safer commute.

Traditional seasonal thinking can encourage attention to these changes. But observation should not harden into a universal prescription. The Northern and Southern Hemispheres have opposite seasons. Coastal and inland communities face different climates. Even neighbors may have unequal access to safe housing, heating, cooling, or flexible working hours.

The better question is not “What must everyone eat on this calendar date?” but “What conditions and risks matter here, now, for this person?”

## The Myth of the Hourly Detox Chart

Some wellness charts divide the day into fixed periods and claim that particular organs detoxify only during their assigned hours. The picture is memorable, but the liver, kidneys, and other systems do not stop working when an imaginary shift ends.

Certain physiological functions do vary over the day. That finding does not establish the accuracy of a traditional organ timetable. A scientific claim would need to specify which substance changes, when, how it is measured, whether the effect is reproducible, and whether it improves meaningful health outcomes.

Without that evidence, such a chart should not guide diagnosis or treatment, and it should not be used to frighten people about the occasional late night.

## Making Time for Life

A rotating-shift nurse, a parent caring for a baby, and an early-start warehouse worker may each need a different practical routine. Regularity is not always a shared clock time. Sometimes it means protecting the most predictable recovery period that circumstances allow.

Small adjustments can help: noticing recurring interruptions, improving the rest environment, and discussing persistent difficulties with appropriate professionals. Other changes require help from employers or family members.

Time also holds conversation, care, companionship, and quiet moments alone. If wellness turns every hour into an optimization task, it risks losing sight of the life it was meant to support.

Understanding biological rhythms should help us make room for living, not turn us into clocks.
