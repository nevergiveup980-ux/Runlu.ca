A Sudden Head-and-Neck Pain: From Worry to Gradual Recovery

A personal experience and reflections on medical care, care at home, and companionship

November 2026, Calgary, Canada.

At first, I felt only a little discomfort at the base of my neck, near my upper back. I assumed I had slept awkwardly and paid little attention. Later, numb, swollen-feeling pain appeared in turn on the left side of my neck, behind and above my ear, and at the back of my head. The location sometimes shifted, and I occasionally felt discomfort even while sitting quietly. My skin was not numb to touch; if anything, it felt unusually sensitive.

I had no obvious fever, cough, or cold symptoms, but I could not tell whether the pain came from muscles, nerves, or something else. The shifting location was especially unsettling: at times it seemed to come from my neck, at others from behind my ear or the back of my head. Was this ordinary muscular discomfort? Was there something else that needed to be ruled out?

I gradually realized that bodily signals cannot always be interpreted accurately by sensation alone. The absence of familiar symptoms does not rule out every problem, and shifting pain does not, by itself, establish a diagnosis.

Two Medical Visits, Questions Remaining

At the first visit, the doctor examined my neck and shoulders, thought the problem might be muscular, and prescribed topical diclofenac. At a second visit, another doctor prescribed doxycycline and a topical cream containing an antifungal agent and a corticosteroid, and arranged blood tests for later.

At the time, I had not fully understood the reasoning behind the second prescription, and I did not use the medications prescribed at either visit. This records my personal decision; it does not mean declining prescribed medication was the right choice or would be appropriate for anyone else. Decisions to start, change, or stop prescription medicines should be discussed with the prescribing clinician or a pharmacist.

Looking back, I see the importance of asking about the suspected diagnosis, the purpose and risks of medication, and follow-up plans. When two visits lead to different approaches, clearly describing when symptoms began, how their location changed, and what medicines or care measures have already been used can help a clinician reassess.

Care at Home and Changes in Symptoms

During my rest, my wife cared for me attentively. Drawing on her experience, she used external traditional Chinese medicine preparations, oral Chinese patent medicines, acupuncture, and massage. I also tried warm showers; one brought noticeable relief, but it did not last long.

Recovery was not something I could capture in a single moment. The earlier discomfort around my neck and upper back gradually eased, and the recurring numb, aching sensations around my head and neck became less frequent. I watched these changes without knowing why the symptoms had appeared or why they were subsiding. By the evening of November 11, I could barely feel anything unusual.

When pain eases, it is tempting to credit the most recent thing we tried. But rest, everyday care, and the natural course of symptoms overlapped. I cannot know what brought relief, and this personal observation cannot establish that any particular treatment works.

Medication Safety Deserves Serious Attention

For a short period, I took Niuhuang Jiedu tablets above the dose recommended in the package instructions. Looking back, this was not something to imitate. Some formulations contain realgar, an arsenic-containing ingredient; excessive use may pose serious risks. Chinese patent medicines also require careful checking of their specific ingredients, instructions, contraindications, and interactions.

Traditional practices can be recorded respectfully, but they cannot replace scientific assessment of medication safety and treatment effectiveness. When the cause of neck pain is unknown, forceful massage or neck manipulation should not be attempted independently.

This experience reminded me that familiarity with a medicine is no reason to increase the dose. Formulations can vary between manufacturers. Anyone who has taken more than the recommended amount should seek individualized advice promptly from a clinician, pharmacist, or local poison information service rather than waiting for obvious symptoms.

Feeling Better Does Not Establish the Cause

There is still no confirmed diagnosis of whether this pain came from muscles, nerves, or another cause. Follow-up testing and medical review remain important when indicated. A sudden severe headache, changes in vision or speech, weakness in a limb, or an unsteady gait require urgent medical attention.

Along the Way, How Fortunate to Have You

What stays with me most from this experience is not a particular treatment, but the knowledge that someone was there to care for me when I was unwell.

Now that my body feels calmer, I remember not only the pain but also the way people support each other through uncertainty. Family care cannot replace professional medical assessment, but it can bring reassurance while we wait, observe, and recover.

When I was younger, I tended to take health for granted. With age, I have come to appreciate how precious an ordinary, peaceful day can be—and how fortunate it is to have someone beside you through life's uncertainties. I can be grateful without calling any care method a miracle.

Along the way, how fortunate to have you.

Personal account · Not medical advice

This is a personal experience without a confirmed clinical diagnosis, not a case report or a recommendation to use medicines, acupuncture, massage, or external traditional preparations. Do not imitate excessive dosing. Persistent or recurrent symptoms, or warning signs, warrant evaluation by a qualified medical professional.
