Fat Is Not the Enemy: Rethinking Oils and Health

Fat is often discussed alongside weight, cholesterol and health warnings. Yet the body needs fat, and different fats deserve different judgments. The more useful questions are what we eat, what we replace, how we cook and which habits we can maintain.

Why the body needs fat

Fat supplies energy, forms part of cell membranes, helps the body absorb vitamins A, D, E and K, and provides essential fatty acids. Calling all fat harmful overlooks its ordinary biological functions.

Fat also provides about nine kilocalories per gram, more than protein or carbohydrate. The aim is not to eat unlimited amounts but to consider food sources, portions and the overall diet.

Not all dietary fats have the same effects

Foods contain different mixtures of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids. Olive and canola oils, nuts, seeds and fish often provide more unsaturated fat, while butter, fatty meats and some processed foods can contain more saturated fat.

Dietary guidance generally emphasizes replacing some saturated fat with unsaturated fat rather than eliminating fat altogether. What replaces a food matters more than claims that one oil is miraculous.

Why industrial trans fat deserves special attention

Partially hydrogenated oils were a major source of industrial trans fat. Higher intake is associated with increased cardiovascular risk. Canada has prohibited partially hydrogenated oils in foods, while regulations vary elsewhere.

Checking ingredient lists and nutrition information can help, although labeling rules differ by region. Small amounts of naturally occurring trans fat in ruminant foods should not be confused with industrial trans fat in source and context.

Essential fatty acids, fish and nuts

The body cannot make certain essential fatty acids. Flaxseed and walnuts provide plant-derived alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), while some fish provide the long-chain omega-3 fatty acids EPA and DHA.

Eating one particular food does not guarantee disease prevention. Decisions about fish-oil supplements depend on diet, health goals, medicines and professional advice; supplements are not universal insurance.

Cooking methods matter too

When choosing cooking oils, consider their fatty acid composition, flavor, cooking temperature and freshness. Repeated high-temperature use can produce degradation products, and visible smoke is a reason to adjust heat and ventilation.

There is no need to find one perfect oil for every dish. Less deep-frying, avoiding repeatedly reused oil and mixing steaming, simmering and stir-frying can be more sustainable than chasing expensive products.

Balance matters more than slogans

Cooking oil, eggs, a handful of nuts and fish all belong in the context of the whole diet. The goal is not to avoid fat completely but to enjoy varied foods in appropriate portions and limit the dominance of highly processed foods.

Healthy eating need not be a list of prohibitions. Learning to choose, enjoy and moderate familiar foods makes habits more realistic for the long term.

RUNLU · Today's practice

  • Notice the sources of fat in your week: cooking oil, nuts, fish, dairy and packaged snacks.
  • Try replacing some foods high in saturated fat with suitable amounts of plant oils, nuts or fish, rather than simply adding calories.
  • Check whether cooking oil is repeatedly used at very high temperatures.

For general education only, not individual medical advice. Consult a qualified professional about dietary changes when managing cardiovascular or metabolic conditions or taking medication.
