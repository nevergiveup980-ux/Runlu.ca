# The Things She Didn't Take
### RUNLU SHORT FICTION — Story 001 · V13 manuscript candidate
**By RUNLU**

## 1. The List

Claire arrived at her father's house at eight twenty with coffee in a cardboard tray and a folder of colored stickers. It was a Saturday in late September, and the first frost had silvered the edges of the grass along the sidewalk. The truck would come on Monday. By six that evening, she intended to have everything sorted.

Arthur opened the door before she knocked.

“You're early,” he said.

“I'm ten minutes late.”

“Then you're early for you.”

He took one coffee, glanced at the folder under her arm, and moved aside. In the hallway, the familiar smell of floor polish had been replaced by cardboard and the faint dust of things disturbed. Two boxes sat beside the stairs, both marked KITCHEN in Arthur's square capitals.

Claire set her bag down. “I've made a system.”

“So have I.”

She opened her folder on the kitchen table. Red meant discard, blue meant donate, green meant take to the apartment. She had printed a room-by-room checklist with empty squares for completion.

Arthur put a folded grocery receipt beside it. On the back, he had written: KEEP, GIVE, GARBAGE.

“Yours has fewer colors,” she said.

“Yours has more paper.”

He smiled, but not quite enough for her to know whether it was a joke.

Arthur had lived in the bungalow for thirty-eight years. Claire had lived there for nineteen of them, then spent the next thirty coming back for birthdays, holidays, and the occasional repair she insisted he should hire someone else to do. When he announced in August that he was moving into a one-bedroom apartment, she had offered to organize everything. He had thanked her. She had taken that as permission to take charge.

“Let's start in the garage,” she said.

Arthur lifted his coffee. “Let's finish this first.”

She looked at the time. Then, because she had promised herself she would not turn the day into an argument before breakfast, she sat down.

## 2. The Garage

The garage held less than Claire expected. Arthur had already given away the lawn mower, the old ladder, and a heavy workbench he had built when she was small. The outlines of their absence remained on the concrete: pale rectangles where sunlight and dust had not reached.

“You should have waited,” Claire said.

“For what?”

“For me. We could have sold some of it.”

“We could have.”

“Dad.”

“The mower went to the family across the lane. The ladder went to a man who needed one.”

“Did he pay you?”

“No.”

She looked at the bare wall. “That was a good ladder.”

“It still is.”

Claire moved a plastic tub toward the driveway. Inside were extension cords, a tin of mismatched screws, and three pairs of work gloves. She could sort them quickly. She was good at making decisions about objects. At work, people thanked her for it.

At the back stood a set of metal shelves, four tiers high, gray paint chipped at the corners. They had held paint cans, winter tires, and bags of soil. She remembered helping Arthur assemble them when she was twelve, though she had mostly complained about the cold.

“These will fit in my basement,” she said.

“They're going tomorrow.”

“Where?”

“To the new neighbor. The woman in the duplex.”

Claire stopped with a roll of tape in her hand. “You gave them away?”

“I said she could have them.”

“But I need shelves.”

“You have shelves.”

“I have some shelves. I could use these.”

Arthur rubbed a finger over the chipped paint. “She has nothing to put her things on.”

Claire heard herself reply, “That doesn't mean we have to solve it.”

Arthur did not answer. He picked up the tin of screws and began separating the usable ones from the rusted.

## 3. The Promise

The neighbor's name was Nadia. She had moved into the duplex in August with a small boy and a rented van that was gone by evening. Arthur had met her while she was carrying a mattress up the front steps.

“She said she'd come at noon,” he told Claire.

“Tomorrow?”

“Today.”

Claire checked her phone. “You said tomorrow.”

“I said they're going tomorrow. I meant they'll be gone by tomorrow.”

She stared at him. “That's not the same thing.”

“No,” he agreed. “It isn't.”

There was no satisfaction in his agreement. That made it harder to continue.

Claire walked to the driveway and looked at the shelves through the open garage door. They were ordinary shelves. She could buy something similar for eighty dollars, maybe less if she waited for a sale. But she disliked having a decision made without her, particularly when she had come to help.

Arthur joined her, carrying the empty coffee cup.

“It's not about the shelves,” she said.

“What is it about?”

She had no answer that would sound reasonable.

A truck passed, throwing a low wash of sound across the street. A school bus stopped at the corner even though it was Saturday, then moved on without anyone getting out.

“She's been making do with boxes,” Arthur said. “She asked if I knew where she could get something sturdy.”

“And you said yes.”

“I had something sturdy.”

Claire folded her arms. “You could have asked me.”

Arthur looked toward the house. “I could have.”

He went inside, leaving the conversation unfinished. Claire disliked unfinished conversations almost as much as decisions made without her.

## 4. The Phone Call

At ten forty, Ethan called.

“Mom, can you take me to the school?”

“Why?”

“We're setting up for the concert. I told you.”

“You said Sunday.”

“It's Saturday. The concert's Sunday.”

Claire closed her eyes. Ethan had been talking about the school music fundraiser for two weeks. He was supposed to help set up chairs and sound equipment, then stay for rehearsal. She had put the concert in her calendar, but not the setup.

“Can't someone else drive you?”

“Maybe. I thought you were coming home at lunch.”

“I can't. I'm at Grandpa's.”

A pause. “Okay.”

She knew that okay. It meant he would ask once, then arrange his disappointment around her schedule.

“Ethan, I have a lot to do.”

“I know.”

“Call your dad.”

“He's working.”

“Then see if somebody from school can pick you up.”

“Sure.”

He hung up politely. That was somehow worse than if he had complained.

Claire returned to the garage. Arthur was kneeling beside a box of old plumbing fittings, putting them into a smaller container.

“Everything all right?” he asked.

“Ethan forgot to tell me something.”

Arthur looked up. “Did he?”

She reached for a red sticker. It clung to her finger instead of the box.

## 5. The Kitchen Drawer

Near noon, Claire went inside for water. The kitchen drawers were mostly empty. In one, Arthur had kept a rubber band around a dozen pencils, a pair of scissors, two batteries of uncertain age, and a small notebook containing measurements for window screens.

She had expected the drawer to be full of forgotten clutter. Instead it held things that had been used and put back.

She picked up the notebook. The first page listed widths and heights. Another had a sketch of the kitchen cabinet hinge. No messages, no private confessions, nothing that explained their family. Just the dimensions of a house Arthur had maintained because it needed maintaining.

“Do you want this?” she called.

Arthur appeared in the doorway. “No. The new place has different windows.”

“You wrote everything down.”

“Not everything.”

She turned another page. “You never told me when something was broken.”

“Usually it wasn't broken for long.”

Claire put the notebook back. When she was young, she had interpreted his silence as lack of interest. Later, she had decided he cared mostly about practical things. She could still remember asking him to come to a school play and hearing him say he would try. He had arrived late, carrying the smell of rain and the municipal maintenance yard on his coat. She remembered the late arrival more clearly than the fact that he came.

Arthur reached past her for the scissors.

“You can throw those batteries out,” he said. “I keep forgetting.”

Claire laughed once, unexpectedly. “That's what I came for. To throw out the batteries.”

“Good. One thing off your list.”

She looked at him. “Why did you decide to move now?”

“I don't want to climb the front steps when there's ice. And I don't need three bedrooms.”

“Are you sure you'll like it?”

“No,” he said. “But I'm sure about leaving here.”

She had spent weeks worrying that he was making the wrong decision. It occurred to her that she had never asked which part of it he was looking forward to.

“What do you want in the new place?” she asked.

“A chair by the window. A table big enough for two.”

“That's all?”

“And somewhere to put my boots.”

He held up the scissors as if that settled the matter, and went back outside.

## 6. The Pickup

Nadia arrived twenty minutes early in a borrowed pickup. Her son sat in the passenger seat with a picture book spread across his knees.

“I'm sorry,” she said when Claire opened the garage. “My friend has to be at work by one. If it's inconvenient, we can come back.”

Arthur stepped forward. “It's ready.”

It wasn't. The shelves still held two paint trays and a cardboard box of hardware. Claire had planned to photograph them, measure the space in her basement, and decide whether to object again.

“We need to empty them,” she said.

Nadia nodded. “I can help.”

Her friend, a man in a baseball cap, brought a moving blanket. The four of them worked without much discussion. The shelves were heavier than they looked. Arthur directed them to lift from the bottom, and Claire steadied the side as they turned through the garage opening.

Nadia's son watched from the truck, lifting his head each time someone said his mother's name.

“Are these for his room?” Claire asked.

“For everything,” Nadia said. “Books, groceries, shoes. Our place is small.”

Claire thought of the shelves in her basement, loaded with half-used paint and Christmas decorations she had not opened in years.

When they had secured the frame, Nadia offered Arthur a folded twenty-dollar bill. He shook his head. She tried again, then slipped the money back into her pocket.

“Thank you,” she said. “Really.”

Arthur nodded toward the truck. “Tie the top as well. It catches wind.”

Nadia smiled. “We will.”

The pickup left slowly. Claire watched the empty rectangle against the garage wall.

She was surprised to find that the absence did not look like a loss.

## 7. The Argument

“Next time,” Claire said, “please tell me before you promise something.”

Arthur was sweeping the floor. “There won't be many next times.”

“You know what I mean.”

“I do.”

“I'm trying to help you.”

“I know that too.”

“Then why do you make it difficult?”

He rested both hands on the broom handle. “Because you keep asking what you should do with my things. You don't ask what I want done.”

Claire opened her mouth, then shut it.

Arthur went on, not angrily. “You made a plan before you saw the house. I made some decisions before you came. Neither of us liked the other one's.”

“I took time off to do this.”

“And I'm grateful.”

“But you don't act grateful.”

“I thought coffee and a thank-you would do it.”

She looked at the folder on the workbench. Red, blue, green. Every object had a destination. The people did not.

“I'm worried about you,” she said.

Arthur's face changed slightly. “I know.”

“You've been here forever.”

“Not forever.”

“What if you hate the apartment?”

“Then I'll be an old man who hates his apartment. I'll manage.”

She laughed despite herself, then felt close to tears and resented that too.

Arthur swept a line of dust toward the driveway. “I didn't give the shelves away to upset you.”

“I know.”

“I gave them away because I was finished using them.”

He said it plainly, without a lesson attached. Claire wondered whether she had been expecting one so she could argue against it.

## 8. The Choice

Her phone vibrated. Ethan had sent a message: *I found a ride. Don't worry.*

She read it twice. Then she called him.

“Did you get there?”

“Not yet. Marcus's mom can take me, but she has to pick up his sister first.”

“I can come.”

“Mom, you're busy.”

“I can come,” she repeated. “I'll be there in twenty-five minutes.”

When she ended the call, Arthur was tying a garbage bag.

“I have to go,” she said.

“Good.”

“Good?”

“You've done plenty.”

“The garage isn't finished.”

“It will be.”

“And the kitchen—”

“I know where the kitchen is.”

Claire picked up her folder, then set it down again. “I can come back tomorrow.”

“Come for lunch,” Arthur said. “We can finish after.”

She hesitated. “What should I bring?”

“Nothing. I'll make sandwiches.”

“Dad, you're moving. You don't have to—”

“Claire.”

She stopped.

“Just come.”

On the drive across Calgary, she watched the city move from older streets into newer subdivisions. She had been taking this route for years, always with an errand at the other end. At a red light she sent Ethan a message: *I'm on my way.*

He replied with a thumbs-up. She wished for something warmer, then remembered he had no reason to expect it yet.

At the school, he came out carrying a coil of cable over one shoulder.

“You didn't have to,” he said, climbing in.

“I know.”

He put the cable on the floor. “Grandpa okay?”

“He's moving.”

“I know that.”

“I think he's okay.”

Ethan looked at her. “Are you?”

She considered saying yes. Instead she said, “I'm working on it.”

He smiled at the windshield, a small smile she might have missed if she had been checking the time.

## 9. The Apartment

Two weeks later, Claire visited Arthur's new apartment. It was on the third floor of a low brick building, with a wide elevator and a window overlooking a courtyard where three young trees had been planted in square beds of gravel.

Arthur had arranged his belongings with almost alarming speed. The table stood beside the kitchen. A lamp she remembered from childhood occupied the corner. There were fewer books than she expected, and no stacks of newspapers.

“Where's the big armchair?” she asked.

“Too wide for the doorway.”

“You loved that chair.”

“I liked sitting in it. That's different.”

She walked to the window. Below, someone had left a bicycle against a railing. The courtyard was not beautiful, but it was bright.

“Have you met anyone?”

“A man on the second floor knows everything about birds.”

“Do you like birds?”

“Not as much as he does.”

Arthur put the kettle on. The sound filled the apartment without making it feel crowded.

Claire noticed a small wooden stool near the window. Its surface was scratched and one leg had a darker patch where Arthur had repaired it years ago.

“You kept that?”

“It fits.”

She wanted to ask whether he had kept it because she used to stand on it to reach the kitchen counter. She wanted the answer to be yes. But Arthur had already told her what he valued: the chair by the window, the table for two, somewhere for his boots. Perhaps the stool was only a stool.

He set out two cups.

“Ethan coming Sunday?” he asked.

“He said he would.”

“Good. I need someone to tell me what all these buttons on the television do.”

“You know how to use a television.”

“Not this one.”

Claire sat at the table. For the first time in weeks, there was no list between them.

## 10. The Space

That evening, Claire went home and carried a box of old household supplies down to her basement. She had intended to find a place for everything, as she always did. Instead she stood in front of a shelving unit and looked at the crowded tiers.

There were paint cans from rooms repainted twice since, a cracked watering can, two sets of holiday lights she had replaced, and a bundle of curtain rods that fit no window in the house.

She took out the things she no longer needed. She did not make a spreadsheet. She put usable items aside for the neighborhood reuse shelf at the community center and carried the rest to the appropriate bins.

Ethan appeared on the stairs.

“Are we moving?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then why are you cleaning?”

“Because I can.”

He came down and held up the curtain rods. “These any good?”

“I think so.”

“Could we give them away?”

“Yes.”

He put them beside the door. Then he stayed to help, not because she asked, but because there was room beside her.

By the time they finished, one shelf stood almost empty. Claire considered filling it with the boxes from the floor. Instead she left them where they were until she knew what they contained.

Upstairs, her phone lit with a message from Arthur: *Found a place for the boots.*

He had attached a photograph of a narrow mat by the apartment door. Two pairs of boots stood on it, neatly side by side.

Claire typed, *Looks good.*

Then she added, *See you Sunday.*

She set the phone down and went back to the basement. Ethan was waiting with a box in his hands, looking for somewhere to put it. Claire moved aside so he could choose.
