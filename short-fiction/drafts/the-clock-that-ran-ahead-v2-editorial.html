The Clock That Ran Ahead

RUNLU SHORT FICTION · Story 001 · By RUNLU

Returning to her father’s Alberta home, Claire finds that the clock he kept eleven minutes fast is still running perfectly. Sorting through his ordinary possessions unsettles the simple story she has told herself about her family.

I. The House

The clock above the fireplace was eleven minutes fast. It had always irritated Claire that her father, who spent his working life checking the condition of municipal vehicles, seemed content to leave this one thing alone.

Claire Bennett noticed it before the dust on the mantel, before the stack of unopened mail, before the slippers tucked neatly beneath her father's chair. She had been in the house for less than a minute and had already found something to correct.

Her phone read 2:43. The clock read 2:54.

Outside, a thin November snow was moving across the street in slanting lines. Claire had driven from Calgary through a sky the colour of unwashed wool. In the town where she grew up, the bakery had become a mortgage office, the hardware store had closed, and the small garage where her father had once taken on weekend repair jobs had a FOR LEASE sign in the window.

Arthur Bennett had been buried twelve days earlier. It was Friday afternoon. The real estate agent wanted photographs of the house by Wednesday. Claire had agreed to a listing date without quite believing the house would be ready.

On the passenger seat lay a folder of forms: an estate inventory, a utility bill, a handwritten list of people to call. Claire had made the list on the evening after the funeral. She had already crossed out the florist and the minister. The list had seemed manageable then. Now each item opened into three others. She could spend a whole day deciding what to do with a single drawer.

Claire left her suitcase by the stairs. She took a dining chair to the fireplace, climbed up, and opened the glass door. The minute hand moved with a little resistance. When she stepped down, the clock showed the correct time.

She stood looking at it. The correction made the room seem less familiar, as though she had moved a piece of furniture in a childhood photograph.

Then she took the chair back to the table and began opening cupboards.

II. The Language Of Repairs

Arthur had a way of showing up with exactly the wrong thing and exactly what was needed.

When Claire was eleven, she wanted a blue bicycle. He brought home a rusted red one from a garage sale. For three evenings she heard the scrape of sandpaper through the garage wall. On Saturday morning the bicycle stood on the driveway, painted a shade of blue so close to the one she had imagined that she was briefly angry he had managed to guess it.

He did not come outside to watch her ride. He was in the garage, putting his tools away.

When she broke her wrist at sixteen, he drove through freezing rain to the hospital with a sweater, a bottle of water, and a paperback novel. He did not ask if she was frightened. He asked if the doctor had checked her fingers for circulation.

At nineteen, as she prepared to leave for university, he inspected her car while her mother stood on the porch trying not to cry. He checked the tires, the washer fluid, the emergency blanket. Then he put a flashlight in the glove compartment.

"Don't run the tank too low in winter," he said.

"I won't."

He closed the door and gave the roof a small pat. Claire waited for him to say he would miss her. He told her the highway might be icy after dark.

She drove away furious with him, and a little furious with herself for having expected anything else.

Halfway to the university, the car began to rattle. She pulled into a gas station and opened the trunk, certain the spare tire had come loose. It was the toolbox Arthur had tucked behind the blanket. A note was taped to the handle: Call when you get there. No signature. She kept the note for years, though she never told him. That afternoon she called from a pay phone outside the residence hall. He asked how the drive had been. She said fine. They spoke for less than two minutes. It was not the conversation she had wanted, but she could remember every word of it.

Her mother, Margaret, had once said, "Arthur thinks love is a problem you solve with a screwdriver."

At thirteen, Claire had found that funny. At thirty, she thought it cruel. In middle age, she could see how both reactions had been incomplete.

III. Two Kinds Of Silence

The house had not always been quiet.

Margaret used to play the radio while she cooked. She sang along badly and confidently, tapping a wooden spoon against the counter. On Saturday mornings she opened every window she could, even when the air outside was cold. Arthur would come in from the garage and shut them again, one by one.

"Fresh air won't hurt you," she would say.

"Heating bill might," he would answer.

They could laugh about that once. Later, they couldn't.

In the last year of their marriage, Margaret took a job at a community arts centre in a neighbouring town. She came home talking about exhibitions, new people, and the possibility of training as an arts administrator. Arthur listened while checking a list of repairs for the municipal fleet. When she asked what he thought, he said the commute would be hard on the car.

He was not trying to be unkind. That was part of what made it so difficult.

Margaret began telling her news to friends first. Arthur began taking extra shifts at the depot. Dinner shifted from six to seven, then became something each of them assembled separately. Their conversations narrowed to bills, weather, and whether the back door had been locked.

Claire saw some of it. At nineteen, she also had a new life waiting, a dorm room, a course schedule, a boy she hoped would call. She was relieved to leave the tension behind and ashamed of that relief.

Three days after she moved out, Margaret took an evening bus west. She had left Arthur a message asking him to meet her at the station before she boarded. Claire did not know about that message then. Claire was told the marriage was over. She built a story around the facts she knew: her mother had tried, her father had refused, and the silence was proof.

Arthur never challenged her version.

There were Christmas visits afterward, at first awkward and then ordinary. Margaret sent cards. Arthur mailed cheques when Claire's car needed work. At her wedding, they stood on opposite sides of the family photograph and managed a civil conversation about the weather. Claire sometimes tried to read the old marriage in their expressions, as though one photograph could explain what two people had lived through. Neither parent encouraged her to do it. She kept trying anyway.

IV. The Workbench

On Saturday, Claire worked in the narrow room behind the kitchen. Arthur's bench stood beneath a north-facing window. Its drawers were labelled in block letters: CONNECTORS, FUSES, BOLTS, GASKETS. A battered multimeter lay beside a roll of electrical tape, and an old inspection lamp rested on a square of felt.

She lifted the inspection lamp, found the switch stiff, and put it down. She knew enough to know she would only make it worse.

Beneath a stack of equipment catalogues she found a blue notebook. Most pages were ordinary records: vehicle numbers, work orders, replacement parts, and notes about faults that returned in cold weather. Between the work orders lay a loose carbon copy, dated the day Margaret left. It recorded a stubborn breakdown at the depot and a final handwritten time: 5:31 p.m. Claire put it aside without thinking much of it. In the back were household notes, equally plain: Call plumber. Renew insurance. Claire's birthday.

Her birthday was underlined. Beside it Arthur had written ask about the new job. Claire could not remember whether he had asked. She could remember being annoyed when he wanted to know what her new position paid, as if that were the only part he understood.

She turned another page. There was a list of measurements for shelves he had promised to build in her first apartment. He had made the shelves. They were still in her basement, holding paint cans and a broken lamp.

Claire sat on the stool. She had come to sort objects into things to keep and things to give away. The notebook was making that distinction unexpectedly difficult.

She remembered the phone call from her father on the night Margaret left. He had asked whether the residence hall was warm enough and whether she had found a parking space. He had not mentioned that his wife was gone.

For years she had taken that omission as evidence he did not care. Looking at his lists now, she wondered whether he had been unable to say what mattered even when it was the only thing on his mind.

V. Thomas’s Garage

Thomas Miller lived two doors down. He answered Claire's knock wearing a faded hockey sweatshirt and holding a mug of coffee. The garage behind him smelled of sawdust and winter tires.

"Your dad used to tell me I kept too many things," Thomas said as he cleared a chair. "Coming from Arthur, that was rich."

Claire had brought the old inspection lamp, hoping Thomas might know whether it was worth keeping. He checked the frayed cord, then set it down.

"That belonged to the depot, I think. He was always taking on one more job."

They talked about the depot, about the former colleagues who had kept calling after Arthur officially retired, about the winters when the furnace failed and the men worked in their coats.

"Did he ever talk about Mum leaving?" Claire asked.

Thomas rested his mug on the bench. "Not in a way that would satisfy you."

"Did he try to stop her?"

"He went to the depot that day. I know that much. A vehicle had broken down. He came over afterward."

"Did they see each other?"

"I don't know. He didn't tell me."

She waited for more, but Thomas only rubbed a thumb along the handle of his mug.

"He was trying to talk," Thomas said at last. "He just kept stepping around the thing he meant to say."

Claire had come looking for certainty. Thomas refused to manufacture it for her.

"He could be a good friend," he said. "He could also be stubborn enough to make you want to throw a wrench at him. Don't let anyone turn him into a saint just because he's gone."

Claire laughed, unexpectedly. It was the first time since the funeral that laughing had not felt disloyal.

VI. Margaret’s Life

Claire called her mother from the kitchen that evening. Margaret lived outside Victoria with her second husband, Daniel, a retired librarian who made elaborate breakfasts and forgot where he left his glasses.

"Thomas said Dad went to the depot when you left," Claire said. "I never knew that."

There was a pause. "Yes. He called that evening. I'd left a message that morning, asking him to come to the station."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you were nineteen, and whether he came to the station wasn't an explanation for a marriage."

Claire ran a finger along a crack in the kitchen table.

"Did he ask you to come back?"

"He said he got there after the bus left. Later, we spent a weekend together that winter. We walked by the harbour. We talked properly for the first time in ages."

"And?"

"We were still ourselves afterward."

Claire waited.

"I wanted to go back to school," Margaret said. "I wanted work that wasn't temporary, and a place where I could start again. Arthur wanted his familiar job, his street, his routines. Neither of those wishes was wrong. But we'd spent years treating the other's wish as a criticism of our own."

"Were you happy with him?"

"Sometimes very happy. Sometimes terribly lonely. That's the trouble with a long life together. More than one thing can be true."

From the other end of the line, Daniel called to ask whether Margaret wanted tea.

"In a minute," she answered, then returned to Claire. "I know you thought you had to choose between us. You didn't. You still don't."

Before hanging up, Margaret asked about the old house. Claire admitted she had accepted an agent's advice to sell quickly, though she had not signed anything. "You don't have to keep a house to keep him," Margaret said. "And you don't have to sell it to prove you've moved on."

After the call, Claire opened the refrigerator. Arthur had left a half-used jar of mustard and a carton of milk that had expired before he died. She threw them away. The ordinary work steadied her. Then she wrote her mother's words on the back of an envelope, not because she needed permission but because she was tired of making decisions as though every choice were a verdict.

VII. The Box Marked Claire

On Sunday morning she found a cardboard box on the top shelf of the hall closet. CLAIRE was written across it in black marker.

Inside were school photographs, a swimming certificate, the program from her graduation, and a clipping about her first promotion. Arthur had underlined her name in pencil. There was a picture of the blue bicycle. Claire was standing beside it with one hand on the handlebars and an enormous gap where her front teeth should have been.

At the bottom was a small envelope containing three photographs she had never seen. In one, Arthur sat in the audience at her high-school concert, his hands folded on his knees. In another, he stood at the edge of her university graduation crowd, half hidden behind a taller man. In the third, he held baby Ethan awkwardly against his shoulder, his face turned away from the camera as if listening for something.

She remembered that graduation. Arthur had left early, or so she had thought. He had told her he had an early shift the next morning. Now she could see he had stayed long enough for the ceremony, long enough to watch from the back.

In the envelope was also a programme from a school play. Claire had played a tree. Her father had missed the opening performance, and she had accused him of caring more about another repair job than about her. On the second night she remembered seeing him near the exit, still wearing his work jacket. She had looked away when the curtain fell. The programme was folded along its centre, softened from being opened more than once.

The pictures did not settle everything. They did not replace the words she had wanted to hear. But they added rooms to the house she thought she knew.

Her phone lit up.

Ethan: How's it going?

Claire: Lots of boxes. Found a photo of you with Grandpa.

Ethan: The one where I'm crying?

Claire smiled.

Claire: Different one. How was your week?

Several minutes passed before he answered.

Ethan: Fine. Got 84 in math.

Claire: That's great. Proud of you.

Ethan: Thanks. You coming home tonight?

She started to type, If I get everything finished. Then she looked at the stacks of boxes and deleted the sentence.

Claire: Yes. I'll be home for supper.

VIII. Eleven Minutes

Before she left, Claire took the clock down from the wall. It was heavier than she expected. She carried it to the workshop, set it on the bench beside her phone.

For the next hour the clock kept pace with her phone. The second hand moved steadily, without losing a beat. She had set the hands to match her phone when she arrived on Friday, and it had kept accurate time ever since. The eleven-minute lead had not been a mechanical fault.

She checked the back for a repair date. There was only a faded manufacturer's label and a price written in pencil, nothing more. No explanation for the eleven minutes. The absence annoyed her more than it should have.

She could imagine Arthur moving the hands ahead after Margaret left. She could just as easily imagine the clock being wrong one morning and his deciding not to bother correcting it. Neither possibility was a fact. She had been asking the house to explain a man who had rarely explained himself while alive.

At the workbench she found a packet of replacement screws he had never opened. On the envelope, in his careful block letters, he had written the size of the screw and the name of the supplier. He had not written what the screws were for. Claire slipped the packet into the drawer where she had found it. Some things in the workshop were simply unfinished jobs, and she did not need to turn each of them into a message.

She turned the clock upright and opened its glass door. Before wrapping it, she moved the minute hand forward eleven minutes. It was how she remembered the room, and she wasn't ready to lose that particular strangeness. She didn't have to know why her father had kept it that way.

She wrapped the clock in a blanket and put it on the back seat of her car. She had no proof that its eleven-minute lead had ever meant anything to Arthur. Keeping it would be her choice, not a discovery about his.

The house could be sold. She would take the clock.

She walked once more through the rooms before locking the door. In the hallway, a pencil mark beside the doorframe showed her height at twelve, thirteen, fourteen. Arthur had written the dates in tiny figures. There was no mark for the year she left for university. She touched the highest line, then stepped away. The house was full of records, but not every important thing had been measured.

IX. The Kitchen

Michael helped carry the boxes into their Calgary house after dark. Ethan appeared at the top of the stairs and came down when he saw the clock.

"Grandpa's weird clock," he said.

"That's the one."

"Still ahead?"

"Eleven minutes."

"Why?"

Claire glanced at the walnut case. "He always kept it that way."

Ethan accepted this with the ease of someone who had known Arthur mostly as a grandfather who repaired broken toys and always had peppermint candies in his coat pocket.

They hung the clock in the kitchen, beside the window. Michael checked the time on his watch.

"Want me to set it?"

"Leave it for now," Claire said.

On Monday, she returned to work. There were emails to answer, a client report to revise, and a meeting that ran twenty minutes late. By evening she was tired and short-tempered. When Ethan began telling her about an argument on his hockey team, she half listened while checking her phone.

"Mom?"

She looked up.

"You didn't hear any of that, did you?"

"I'm sorry. Start again."

He gave her a doubtful look. She put the phone facedown on the counter. This time he told the story all the way through. It was not a particularly important story: two players had disagreed about who should take a penalty shot. Ethan had found the whole thing ridiculous. Claire laughed at the right moment, not because she had planned to but because she had actually heard him.

Later, while Ethan was looking for his hockey schedule, Claire noticed the blue notebook on the kitchen counter. She had brought it home without deciding whether to keep it. He flipped through a few pages and asked why Grandpa had written down so many names. "They were people who trusted him to fix what had stopped working," she said. Ethan nodded as if that were explanation enough. He did not ask about September. Claire found herself relieved, then vaguely disappointed, and was surprised by both feelings.

Ethan left his hockey schedule on the counter.

The clock ticked above the window, eleven minutes ahead.

Neither of them looked at it.

X. An Ordinary Saturday

Three weeks later, Margaret came to Calgary for a visit. She stood in the kitchen, still wearing her coat, and looked at the clock.

"Arthur's," she said.

"Yes."

"It used to keep perfect time," Margaret said. "Arthur was very particular about that."

"I don't know when he started keeping it fast," Claire said.

Margaret looked at the hands for a moment. "Neither do I. I remember my bus, though. Five twenty. I was so sure he'd be there."

Claire said nothing. In the kitchen drawer, the blue notebook lay beneath a stack of takeaway menus. She remembered a loose slip inside it, but not what had been written there.

They made tea. Margaret told Claire about a neighbour who had planted tulips too early and a class she was taking in watercolour painting. She had brought photographs of her latest work. Claire spread them on the table, and for once did not feel compelled to ask another question about 2001.

"Are you going to keep the clock fast?" Margaret asked as she put the photographs away.

"I don't know."

"You can set it right, you know. He won't mind."

Claire looked at the clock. "I think I know that now."

Ethan came in from the garage, asking whether anyone had seen his gloves. Margaret told him to look in his jacket pockets. He said he'd already looked. She told him to look again. He found them there.

The three of them laughed, and Claire put the kettle back on.

Margaret took off her coat at last and settled at the table. Claire went to the kitchen drawer for a pencil and found the blue notebook beneath the takeaway menus. A carbon copy slipped out. She caught it before it fell, and saw the final entry in her father's square handwriting: 5:31 p.m.

Five twenty, Margaret had said.

Claire looked toward the clock above the window. She had carried it all the way from Arthur's house without understanding why she wanted its hands left as they were. Now she had a possibility, not a confession: a broken-down vehicle, a bus already gone, and a man who could repair almost anything except the moment he had missed.

She slid the paper back into the notebook. Her mother was waiting at the table. Ethan was coming in from the garage. Claire put the notebook away and joined them.

Ethan began describing a school project. He wanted to build a small model bridge and had already decided which materials would fail. Claire offered to help him test them. "You don't have to," he said, not looking up. "I know," she answered. "I'd like to." He considered that, then pushed his sketchbook toward her.

The three of them bent over the page. Margaret pointed out that the supports looked too thin. Ethan argued that they were only a drawing. Claire reached for a pencil.

Her work phone lit up beside the sketchbook. Claire saw the caller's name, then turned the screen facedown without answering.

"Try a wider base," she said.

Ethan moved his ruler. Margaret held the paper steady.

Above the window, the clock kept ticking, eleven minutes ahead.
