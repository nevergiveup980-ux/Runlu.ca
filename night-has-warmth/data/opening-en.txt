## Chapter One · The Rain Stopped After Nine

Lin Lan first met Cheng Ye on the last Thursday of November.

Rain had begun falling over the city at three that afternoon. At first it was only a thin mist on the glass, but by the time the curatorial meeting ended, the whole riverfront had been pressed by rain into a wavering field of lights. People crowded outside the hotel’s revolving door waiting for rides, phone screens lighting up one after another, then going dark again.

Lin Lan stood in the lobby near the exit with the meeting papers tucked under one arm. At forty-two, she had learned to leave exactly the right amount of distance between herself and other people: not enough to seem cold, not so little that anyone might mistake her for wanting conversation. But that evening the wind pushed through the gap in the door, and one sheet slipped from her folder, skimming across the wet floor toward the street.

Someone stepped on the corner before she could reach it.

“Yours?”

The man bent down and picked it up, but did not hand it back at once. First he brushed the beads of water from the edge with the back of his hand. He moved slowly, as if it were not a meeting agenda that could be printed again at any time, but something worth handling with care.

“Thank you.” Lin Lan reached for it.

His fingers did not touch hers. They paused only for a moment at the other end of the page. Looking back later, Lin Lan knew that nothing had actually happened in that instant. No spark, no music swelling as it would in a film. She had simply caught the faint scent of cedar mingled with rain.

“Has your car arrived?” he asked.

Lin Lan looked at her phone. The ride-hailing app was still searching for a driver. The estimated wait had jumped from eight minutes to seventeen.

“Probably not for a while.”

The man opened his umbrella and said it was easier to get a car on the parking-lot side. He did not ask whether she wanted to come with him. He only tilted the umbrella slightly toward her. The invitation left unspoken was somehow easier to accept.

They stepped into the rain. The umbrella was not small, yet both of them instinctively kept to the edges, leaving between them a narrow strip of space that was almost excessively polite. The wind drove the rain sideways, and Lin Lan’s right shoulder was soon wet. When the man noticed, he shifted the umbrella toward her and let half his own sleeve fall into the rain.

“You don’t have to do that,” she said.

“Do what?”

“Get yourself soaked.”

“My car is close.”

That later proved not to be true. They walked around the side entrance of the hotel and waited nearly twenty minutes by the parking-lot exit. Rain tapped against the umbrella, making silence feel natural. They talked about the old-city renewal meeting that had just ended, about the abandoned cinema on the south side, and about how a building might preserve memory without turning into nostalgia.

“Are you an architect?” Lin Lan asked.

“Restoration,” he said. “Cheng Ye.”

“Lin Lan.”

When she gave her name, he nodded once without repeating it. She liked the restraint in that response, warm enough without trying to become familiar.

At last the car stopped at the curb. Cheng Ye opened the rear door for her and still did not ask for her number. After Lin Lan got in, she looked back and saw him standing in the rain, checking the license plate. He did not turn away until the car had cleared the intersection.

The driver asked, “Your husband isn’t coming?”

“No.”

She answered too quickly. The driver glanced at her in the rearview mirror and asked nothing more.

The normal route home took twenty-three minutes. Lin Lan asked the driver to make an extra loop along the river. She told herself the main road would be congested, though the map showed green all the way. Outside the window, streetlights stretched thin in the puddles. She pulled the rain-dampened agenda from her folder. A pale mark remained on one corner where the sole of Cheng Ye’s shoe had pressed it down.

Zhou Heng was slicing ginger in the kitchen.

“Why so late?” he asked without turning. “Meeting run over?”

“The rain was heavy. Hard to get a car.”

It was not a lie. Precisely because it was not, Lin Lan felt as though she had hidden something.

Zhou Heng pushed a cup of hot water toward her. They had been married nineteen years. He remembered that rain often gave her a headache, and he remembered that there could be only two slices of ginger in the water; a third made it too sharp for her. Lin Lan wrapped her hands around the cup and felt warmth return slowly to her fingertips.

“Did they decide on the south-side cinema project?” he asked.

“Not yet. I’m going to see the site next week.”

“You’ve wanted that one for a long time. You’ll probably get it.”

His voice was sincere and calm. Lin Lan looked at the man with whom she had shared nineteen years of life and suddenly felt a guilt that seemed to come from nowhere. She did not yet know what she was guilty of.

By eleven, Zhou Heng was asleep. Lin Lan lay with her back to him, listening to the last of the rain run from the eaves. She thought about the deliberate strip of space beneath the umbrella, about Cheng Ye’s half-soaked sleeve, and about the fact that he had asked her for nothing.

Because he had asked for nothing, those twenty minutes remained in her body far longer than they should have.

## Chapter Two · The Number Never Sent

Three days later, Lin Lan received a kraft-paper envelope at the exhibition hall’s front desk.

Inside were preliminary drawings for the restoration of the south-side cinema and an old movie ticket. The ticket had faded to a grayish blue. A phone number was written on the back, with no name and no explanation.

She recognized Cheng Ye’s handwriting at once. His entry on the meeting sign-in sheet looked the same: the numbers leaning slightly to the right, the final stroke ending with steady control.

“Who brought this?” she asked the receptionist.

“A courier. He said the drawings needed your signature.”

Lin Lan slipped the ticket between the plans, then took it out again in the elevator back to her office. The last four digits were 0917, her mother’s birthday. She knew it was only a coincidence, but at certain moments coincidence behaved like an intrusive narrator, taking an ordinary event and telling you that it meant more.

She entered the number into her phone without saving it.

The words New Contact appeared at the top of the screen, and beneath them waited an empty space for a name. She stared at that blank field until the elevator doors opened and a colleague behind her reminded her that they had arrived.

That afternoon, the project lead confirmed that the cinema restoration was officially moving forward. Cheng Ye’s firm would handle the architecture; Lin Lan would be responsible for the interior exhibition and the opening program. She ought to call him. It was necessary for work. The explanation was so reasonable it was almost suspicious.

She dialed the number and hung up before the first ring.

The phone did not ring back. She felt relieved, and then faintly disappointed.

That evening, their daughter video-called from Vancouver. Twenty-year-old Zhou Yao complained that the dorm heating was too dry, then showed them the secondhand desk lamp she had just bought. Zhou Heng sat beside Lin Lan and earnestly explained how to check the plug. The three of them argued through the screen for ten minutes and ended up laughing together.

For that moment, the family was complete beyond reproach.

After the call, Zhou Heng handed the phone back to her and asked, “Something on your mind today?”

“The project was approved. I’m just tired.”

“That’s good news.” He reached over and kneaded the back of her neck. “Don’t carry everything by yourself again.”

Lin Lan lowered her head. The gesture was so familiar that in nineteen years she had almost stopped noticing that his palm was still warm. Suddenly she understood that cracks in a life did not always appear because kindness had disappeared. Sometimes the opposite was true. The kindness was still there, the care was still there; only two people had gone so long without truly seeing each other.

That night she pressed the movie ticket into the half-read book beside the bed. Just before she fell asleep, her phone lit up.

A message from an unknown number read: “Sorry. The number on the back of the ticket is the site duty number. I was afraid you wouldn’t be able to reach me.”

Lin Lan read it twice.

The explanation made sense. It did not explain why the number had been written on a movie ticket twenty years old.

She typed: “Got it. Thank you.”

Then deleted it.

She typed: “See you at the site next week.”

Deleted that too.

In the end, she sent only a period.

Cheng Ye did not ask what she meant. A few minutes later, he sent a period back.

Two solitary marks of punctuation rested on their screens across the city, like two people standing behind separate doors. Neither had opened the door, yet each already knew the other was there.

## Chapter Three · The Old Cinema

The south-side cinema was built in 1958.

The first time Lin Lan walked inside, the screen had already been removed, the seats were buried under thick dust, and a dark water stain spread across one corner of the ceiling. Winter sunlight slipped through tall windows that had been half boarded over, illuminating dust as it rose and fell slowly in the air.

Cheng Ye stood in front of the stage wearing a hard hat, checking the load-bearing structure with the site supervisor. He saw her and merely raised one hand to signal that he needed a moment. His concentration belonged so completely to the work that the twenty minutes in the rain seemed never to have happened.

Lin Lan felt reassured by that—and, to her own discomfort, a little disappointed.

“It’s much older than it looks on the plans,” she said.

“People are usually older than they look in photographs too.” Cheng Ye handed her a hard hat. “At least buildings don’t mind having their cracks seen.”

They followed the side corridor toward the projection room. The wooden staircase was rotting. Cheng Ye went ahead, testing each step before putting his weight on it. At the landing, he reached out to shine his light on a broken tread for her.

Lin Lan steadied herself against the wall, and her wrist touched his.

Neither moved away at once.

The contact lasted no more than a second. There was clothing between their skin; it could hardly even be called warmth. But both of them noticed that second at the same time, and so it acquired a length it should not have had.

Cheng Ye pulled his hand back first. “Careful.”

“Mm.”

Two abandoned projectors still remained in the booth. Cheng Ye pushed open a warped iron window. Cold wind poured in, carrying the smell of coal smoke and snow from the old district.

“What’s the hardest thing to restore?” Lin Lan asked.

“Memory.”

“You can work on memory like a building?”

“No.” He looked down at the empty auditorium. “You can only try not to damage it.”

Lin Lan stepped beside him. From up there, the stage, seats, side doors and corridors had shrunk into a single sleeping whole. It occurred to her that perhaps a marriage was like a building used for too long: the structure remained sound, the water and electricity still worked, but certain rooms gradually lost their purpose until the people living there forgot what those rooms had once been for.

“Why old buildings?” she asked.

Cheng Ye was quiet for a moment. “A new building only has to imagine the future. An old one also has to answer to the past.”

He did not look at her when he said it.

The contractor sent lunch in paper boxes—sandwiches already gone cold. Everyone ate standing in the lobby. Cheng Ye stayed several people away from her and spoke only about schedules and fire code. After the meeting, Lin Lan realized she had left her scarf in the projection room.

She went back upstairs alone.

Cheng Ye was standing by the window with the scarf draped over his arm, as if he had known she would return.

“Do you lose things often?” he asked.

“Almost never.”

“Then maybe today you didn’t forget it.”

Lin Lan looked at him. His tone was not flirtatious; he was not even smiling. That was exactly why the sentence could not be dismissed as a joke.

“What do you think it was?”

“Maybe this place wanted you to come back one more time.”

He held the scarf out but kept one end in his hand. A length of soft wool stretched between them like a rope that could be drawn tight or released at any moment.

Lin Lan let go first.

The scarf remained in Cheng Ye’s hand.

“Give it back to me at the next meeting,” she said.

Walking down the stairs, she knew she had just made the first decision she could no longer explain as work.
